Monmouth’s Rebellion and the Battle of Sedgemoor (6 July 1685)

Summary

The Monmouth Rebellion, also known as the “Pitchfork Rebellion,” was an attempt by James Scott, Duke of Monmouth, the illegitimate son of Charles II, to seize the throne from his Catholic uncle, James II. Landing at Lyme Regis in Dorsetshire on 11 June 1685 with just three ships and fewer than a hundred followers, Monmouth raised the Protestant West Country in arms. Within four days he had assembled over two thousand men. The rebellion culminated in a desperate night attack on the royal army at Sedgemoor in the early hours of 6 July 1685 — the last pitched battle fought on English soil. Monmouth’s defeat, his botched execution on Tower Hill, and the subsequent Bloody Assizes under Lord Chief Justice Jefferies, in which over 250 rebels were executed and hundreds more transported, became a byword for Stuart vengeance and did much to prepare the ground for the Glorious Revolution three years later.

The Duke of Monmouth

James Scott, Duke of Monmouth (1649–1685), was the eldest of Charles II’s many illegitimate children, born to Lucy Walters during the king’s exile on the Continent. Handsome, charming, and militarily brave — he had distinguished himself at the siege of Maastricht in 1673 and commanded the English contingent in the French service — Monmouth became the darling of the Protestant faction during the Exclusion Crisis of 1679–1681. The Earl of Shaftesbury and the Whig party promoted the fiction that Charles had secretly married Lucy Walters, the proof of which was supposedly kept in a mysterious black box. Charles himself, pressed by his brother James, issued a declaration in council denying any such marriage and affirming Monmouth’s illegitimacy. After the failure of the Rye House Plot in 1683, in which Monmouth was implicated, he fled into exile in Holland and later Brussels. Upon James’s accession in February 1685, Monmouth was persuaded against his better judgment to mount a desperate invasion of England.

The Landing at Lyme Regis

Monmouth sailed from Holland with three small ships, accompanied by a handful of exiled Whig gentlemen including Lord Grey of Warke and the Scottish republican Fletcher of Salton. He landed at Lyme Regis in Dorsetshire on 11 June 1685 with scarcely a hundred followers. His manifesto, drafted by the republican theorist Robert Ferguson, denounced James II — referred to throughout as the “Duke of York” — as a traitor, a tyrant, an assassin, and a popish usurper, imputing to him the Fire of London, the murder of Sir Edmund Bury Godfrey, and even the poisoning of Charles II.

The response in the West Country was immediate and fervent. Within four days Monmouth had assembled over two thousand men, mostly clothworkers, farmers, artisans, and Dissenters — “the lowest of the people,” in Hume’s dismissive phrase, but men with strong Protestant convictions and old scores against the Stuart court. The Duke of Albemarle, son of the man who had restored Charles II, assembled the Devonshire militia at Axminster, but finding his troops bore a marked affection for Monmouth, prudently retired.

The Rising in the West

Monmouth advanced through the West Country, passing through Taunton, which received him with open arms. Twenty young women of some rank presented him with a pair of silk colours of their own making, together with a Bible. It was at Taunton that Monmouth was persuaded — reluctantly, according to some accounts — to assume the title of king and to assert the legitimacy of his birth. His army swelled to some six thousand men, though many had to be dismissed for want of arms.

The rebellion was not, however, joined by any man of substantial rank or property. The gentry held aloof. The city of London, where a simultaneous rising had been projected, failed to stir. And from Scotland came news that the Earl of Argyll, Monmouth’s confederate, had already been defeated and captured. Monmouth sank into despondency, at one point resolving to abandon his followers; it was the West Countrymen themselves who urged him on.

The Royal Army

James II acted with energy and decision. Parliament voted a bill of attainder against Monmouth and a supply of 400,000 pounds. Six regiments of British troops were recalled from the Dutch service and dispatched to the West Country under the command of Louis de Duras, Earl of Feversham, a French-born Huguenot of limited military ability, and his second-in-command, Colonel John Churchill — the future Duke of Marlborough, whose later desertion of James would prove decisive in the Glorious Revolution. The royal army, numbering some three thousand regulars, took up a position on Sedgemoor, a flat, marshy plain about three miles south-east of Bridgewater.

The Night March and the Battle of Sedgemoor

On the evening of 5 July, Monmouth climbed the tower of St Mary’s Church in Bridgewater and, with the aid of a spyglass, observed the royal camp at Sedgemoor. The horse and foot lay at some distance from each other, carelessly encamped in overconfidence. Monmouth resolved on a night attack.

His plan was bold but simple: he would lead the infantry against Feversham’s foot, while Lord Grey, with the cavalry, would make a circuit to attack the royal horse in a village to the rear and set it ablaze. The rebels marched out in the dead of night, guided by a local man. But the plan unravelled. A Captain Hucker fired a pistol prematurely, alerting the royal camp, though the royalists were at first uncertain what the alarm meant. The rebels found a ditch — the Bussex Rhine — before the royal lines that they had not anticipated, but forced their way across it.

What followed was an extraordinary three-hour contest in the dark. Monmouth’s irregulars — armed with scythes, pitchforks, and a motley collection of firearms — drove the veteran troops back, fighting with a courage that astonished both sides. Hume writes that they “threw the veteran forces into disorder; drove them from their ground; continued the fight till their ammunition failed them; and would at last have obtained a victory, had not the misconduct of Monmouth and the cowardice of Gray prevented it.” Dalrymple’s account describes how the rebels formed into a solid body, “laying their shoulders close to each other,” advancing, stopping, fighting, and dying together. For three hours they sustained the attack.

But Lord Grey’s cavalry charge failed utterly; Grey fled almost at once. Without cavalry support, and with Feversham’s cannon brought to bear on the densely-packed rebel ranks, the advantage turned. The royal horse, having driven off Grey, returned and fell on the rebel rear. When their ammunition ran out, the rebels finally broke. About fifteen hundred fell in the battle and pursuit.

The Capture and Execution of Monmouth

Monmouth fled the field, riding twenty miles until his horse foundered. He exchanged clothes with a peasant and attempted to hide, but was discovered two days later near Ringwood in Dorsetshire, lying in a ditch covered with fern. In his pockets were found some green peas — all that had sustained him — and his George of diamonds. When seized, Hume records, he burst into tears.

He wrote abject letters to James, begging for an audience and hinting at secrets of great importance. James admitted him to his presence, hoping to extract a confession of his accomplices, but Monmouth would not name names. He offered to change his religion; James was unmoved. On 15 July 1685, Monmouth was beheaded on Tower Hill. The execution was a horror: he warned the executioner not to botch the job as had been done with Lord Russell. The warning unnerved the axeman, who struck feebly. Monmouth raised his head from the block and looked reproachfully at him. It took five blows to sever the head. The crowd, which had loved him, dissolved in weeping.

The Bloody Assizes

The vengeance that followed was calculated to terrify. Colonel Percy Kirke, whose regiment — known as “Kirke’s Lambs” — had served at Tangiers, hanged nineteen prisoners without trial at Bridgewater, often while he and his officers drank the king’s health. Feversham hanged another twenty. The country was strewn with the heads and limbs of the executed.

But the worst was yet to come. Lord Chief Justice George Jefferies, the most feared judge in England, was sent on a judicial progress through the West Country — the “Bloody Assizes.” At Dorchester, he exhorted thirty prisoners to confess, and when twenty-nine were found guilty, ordered them to immediate execution. At Exeter, two hundred and forty-three were tried; at Taunton and Wells, more still. Jefferies bullied juries, browbeat witnesses, and harangued the accused with drunken fury. In all, some two hundred and fifty-one men and women were executed by judicial sentence, and hundreds more were transported to the plantations in the West Indies.

The most notorious cases were those of two women: Lady Alice Lisle, the elderly widow of a regicide, condemned for harbouring two rebels the day after Sedgemoor and beheaded at Winchester; and Elizabeth Gaunt, an Anabaptist who had sheltered a rebel, who was burned alive at Tyburn after the man she had saved betrayed her to save his own skin.

Aftermath and Significance

The rebellion, though brief and ill-conducted, had far-reaching consequences. James II, emboldened by his easy victory and by the compliant parliament that had supported him, now believed he could impose his Catholicising policies without resistance. The Bloody Assizes had terrorised the nation but also disgusted it; the executions alienated the very classes — the Protestant gentry and the Dissenting interest — whose support James would desperately need three years later when William of Orange landed at Torbay. As Hume observes, “This victory, obtained by the king in the commencement of his reign, would naturally, had it been managed with prudence, have tended much to increase his power and authority. But by reason of the cruelty with which it was prosecuted, and of the temerity with which it afterward inspired him, it was a principal cause of his sudden ruin and downfal.”

The Battle of Sedgemoor holds a unique place in English history: it was the last pitched battle fought on English soil. It also provided an early display of the military talents of John Churchill, who would go on to become the greatest general of his age. For the common people of the West Country, the rebellion became a folk memory of courage and betrayal — a lost cause in which the scythe and the pitchfork had almost, but not quite, overcome the king’s redcoats.

Contemporary Accounts

Hume’s History of England (vol. 8) provides the most comprehensive narrative of the rebellion, the battle, and the Bloody Assizes, with vivid descriptions of the atrocities committed by Kirke and Jefferies. Dalrymple’s Memoirs of Great Britain and Ireland (vol. 1) adds important tactical detail on the battle of Sedgemoor itself, drawn from the original papers in the state paper office, including Monmouth’s movements in the West and his council of war at Bridgewater. Burnet’s History of His Own Time (vol. 1) offers the Whig perspective, with particular attention to the character of Jefferies and the political consequences of the rebellion.
