Battle of the Boyne (1 July 1690)

Summary

The Battle of the Boyne, fought on 1 July 1690 (Old Style) on the banks of the River Boyne near Drogheda, was the decisive engagement of the Williamite War in Ireland. King William III, commanding a multinational army of some 36,000 men, defeated the forces of the deposed King James II, who fielded approximately 26,000 troops including a contingent of French regulars. The battle secured the Protestant interest in Ireland, confirmed the Glorious Revolution settlement, and sent James fleeing back to France. It has been commemorated in Irish Protestant folk memory — under the anomalous date of 12 July — as the foundational victory of the Orange tradition.

The Williamite War in Ireland

When James II fled England in December 1688, Ireland remained his best hope of recovering the throne. The Lord Deputy, the Earl of Tyrconnel, had systematically purged the Irish administration and army of Protestants and replaced them with Catholics. By the spring of 1689, James’s viceroy held all of Ireland except the Protestant strongholds of Derry and Enniskillen. James himself arrived at Kinsale in March 1689 with French money, French arms, and a corps of French officers, and summoned a Jacobite parliament in Dublin that reversed the Cromwellian land settlement and vested the property of absent Protestants in the crown.

The relief of Derry in July 1689 and Marshal Schomberg’s subsequent campaign in Ulster had checked the Jacobite advance but produced no decisive result. Through the winter of 1689–1690 the two armies faced each other across a wasted countryside, disease taking a heavier toll than combat. It became clear that only the presence of William himself could galvanise the war to a conclusion.

William’s Expedition

William resolved to take personal command of the Irish campaign in the spring of 1690. He assembled an army of some 36,000 men, drawn from English, Dutch, Danish, Brandenburg, and Huguenot regiments — the most formidable force ever sent to Ireland from England. On 11 June 1690 he embarked at Chester with his forces on board 288 transports, escorted by a squadron of six men-of-war under Sir Cloudesley Shovel. Campbell’s Naval History records that William landed at Carrickfergus on 14 June and dismissed Admiral Shovel with orders to join the grand fleet.

William advanced rapidly south from Belfast. He covered in six days a march that had taken Schomberg weeks the previous summer. The Jacobites abandoned the strong passes of Newry and Dundalk without a fight, falling back to the line of the Boyne. Burnet notes that James and his court had been so buoyed by news of political distractions in England that they flattered themselves William would never dare leave England at all — and that James remained ignorant of William’s landing for six days after it occurred.

The Armies

William’s army, encamped on the north bank of the Boyne near Drogheda, numbered about 36,000 men: English, Scots, Dutch, Danes, Brandenburgers, and Huguenot refugees — the latter burning to avenge themselves on the French troops serving under James. The army was well-provisioned, in good spirits, and confident in its commander.

James’s army, on the south bank, numbered about 26,000. His cavalry was good, and he had 5,000 French regulars under the Count of Lauzun, sent by Louis XIV in exchange for 5,000 Irish recruits shipped to France. The Irish infantry were numerous but poorly armed and untrained; their officers were divided by faction and jealousy. The French professionals held the Irish in contempt, and the Irish resented the French.

The Council of War

James held councils of war on 29–30 June. The unanimous advice of both his French and Irish officers was to avoid a general engagement. They urged him to strengthen the garrisons, fall back behind the Shannon, and draw William away from his fleet and supplies, protracting the war until the French navy could cut William’s communications with England. Lauzun’s officers, in particular, argued that the French fleet would soon destroy William’s transports in the Irish Sea, trapping his army in a hostile country.

James overruled them all. He was positive that he must stand and defend the Boyne. To abandon Dublin without a battle, he argued, would lose him all reputation, dishearten his friends in England, and cause the Irish people to capitulate. Burnet records that James seemed almost pleased at the prospect of a decisive battle for his crown: many about him suspected he was weary of the struggle and longed to see an end of it at any rate.

The Ground

The River Boyne at the fords near Drogheda was deep and rose high with the tide. On the south bank, where James’s army lay, the ground rose from the river through a series of defensive positions: first a broken bank lined with old houses and hedges, then a range of small hills, and finally the village of Dunore on a height commanding the whole field. On James’s left, a bog made the ground impassable; on his right, downstream, lay Drogheda itself, held by his garrison. Three miles above the Jacobite camp stood the bridge of Slains, the only crossing beyond the fords. The position was naturally strong, and James had thrown up breastworks at the principal fords.

William’s Wounding

On the afternoon of 30 June, William rode along the north bank to reconnoitre the fords. He stopped for some time in one place to observe the ground. The Jacobites spotted him, brought down two field-pieces concealed behind a hedge, and fired. A ball struck William on the right shoulder, tearing his clothes and about a hand’s-breadth of the skin.

Burnet, who gives a first-hand account, writes that about a spoonful of blood came from the wound and that was all the harm it did. But the terror it struck into those around him was immense. William declared it was nothing, allowed it to be washed and plastered, and immediately remounted. He rode about all the posts of his army — remaining nineteen hours on horseback that day — to dispel the alarm. A deserter carried news of the wound to the Jacobites, and from there it reached France, where it was taken for granted that William could not survive. Paris erupted in rejoicing: the guns of the Bastille were fired, the city was illuminated, and public rejoicings exceeded those for any victory. The French court was later covered in confusion when they learned William was still alive.

The Battle: 1 July 1690

William’s plan, which he communicated to his generals only in written orders late on the night of 30 June, involved a three-pronged assault. Count Schomberg, son of the Marshal, was to march upstream at daybreak with the right wing of about 10,000 men (mostly cavalry), cross at the fords below Slains bridge, and push for the pass of Duleek behind the Jacobite position, cutting off any retreat. Some hours later, once Count Schomberg was known to be across, the centre under the old Duke of Schomberg was to force the fords directly between the two camps. William himself, commanding the left wing, would cross downstream near Drogheda and fall on the Jacobite flank.

The Right Wing: Count Schomberg

Count Schomberg’s column marched off at six in the morning. James, seeing this movement, mistook it for a general advance and sent successive bodies of troops to oppose it, weakening his centre. The Count found little opposition at the crossing, and though the bog on the far side slowed his infantry, his boldness dismayed the enemy, who fell back towards Duleek.

The Centre: The Duke of Schomberg

When William received word that Count Schomberg was across, he ordered the centre to advance. The Dutch Blue Guards entered the river first, followed by the Brandenburghers, prompted by national rivalry. The sudden resistance of so many men swelled the river; the infantry crossed breast-high, holding their arms above their heads, while the horses swam.

The Irish troops fled from the breastworks and hedges after firing only confused, ill-aimed volleys. But as the Williamite infantry emerged into the open ground, General Hamilton — who had commanded James’s cavalry and had previously deserted from William — launched a furious countercharge. He had distributed brandy to his dragoons and led them with a rage that was more frantic than brave. At the same time the French regiments, who had been hidden among the hills, rose up and added their fire.

The double shock threw William’s centre into disorder. The Dutch stopped; the Huguenots were broken through; the Danes turned and fled back across the river. Marshal Schomberg, seeing the crisis, rode into the river to rally the French Protestants, crying out, “Voila, Messieurs, vos persécuteurs!” (“There, gentlemen, are your persecutors!”). In the confusion of the melee, Schomberg was wounded and then killed — most likely by the fire of his own men in the swirling action. Burnet compares his fall to that of Epaminondas, dying on the day of his side’s triumph.

William’s Crossing

William himself had crossed downstream with his cavalry. With his wounded arm freed from its sling and his sword drawn, he appeared on the flank of the Jacobite position at the critical moment. The Jacobites fell back to the strong station of Dunore, where the battle raged for another half-hour with various success. In the heat of the action, one of William’s own dragoons mistook him and clapped a pistol to his head. The King turned it aside and said calmly, “What, do you not know your friends?”

The Rout

The Irish infantry at length gave way. Hamilton’s cavalry attempted one more charge and almost succeeded, but Hamilton himself was taken prisoner. James, who had watched the entire battle from a safe distance at the church of Dunore, seeing that all was lost and that Count Schomberg was still advancing towards Duleek, abandoned his post while the armies were still fighting. He left orders for the army to fall back to the Shannon and fled himself.

Burnet’s account is blunt: James was the first that ran for it and reached Dublin before the action was quite over. He rode that same day from Dublin to Duncannon Fort, and from there boarded a French ship that had been prepared for him by his special direction to Sir Patrick Trant. He sailed for France, never to return to Ireland.

Aftermath and Significance

The Jacobite army lost about 2,000 killed; William’s losses were under 500. William declined a hot pursuit, judging that his cavalry were exhausted and that the pursuit of a broken army would be mere butchery — a thing he had always abhorred. The Irish army, abandoned by its officers, dispersed; many of them reassembled later under the walls of Limerick, where the war would drag on for another year.

The strategic significance of the Boyne was decisive. It secured Dublin and the eastern provinces; it shattered the myth of Jacobite invincibility; it confirmed the Protestant succession in Ireland. For William, the victory justified his bold strategy of leaving England at a moment when political discontent was high and the French fleet dominated the Channel. Burnet notes that the victory was almost wholly attributed to the King’s courage and conduct.

Irish Cultural Memory

The Battle of the Boyne acquired a symbolic resonance far exceeding its military importance. In Irish Protestant folk memory, it became the central episode of the Williamite deliverance — the triumph of Protestant liberty over Catholic tyranny. The date of the battle, 1 July 1690 under the Old Style Julian calendar then in use, translates to 11 July under the New Style Gregorian calendar. But the commemorative celebrations were moved to 12 July after the calendar reform of 1752, giving rise to “Orangement’s Day” or “the Twelfth.” The Orange Order, founded in 1795 to uphold the Protestant ascendancy, took the Boyne as its foundational myth. To this day, the Twelfth of July is marked by parades, bonfires, and marching bands across Northern Ireland — a living, and often contentious, link to the events of 1690.

Contemporary Accounts

Burnet, the Whig bishop, is the most valuable first-hand witness among the sources listed. He does not claim to have been on the field — he was in England at the time — but he wrote his account from conversations with the King and with officers who fought, and he supplies details that appear in no other narrative: the size of the armies, the tenor of James’s council of war, the manner of William’s wounding, and the King’s movements on the battlefield. Dalrymple’s Memoirs draws on original documents and on Story’s contemporary history of the Williamite war, and provides the most detailed tactical account of the battle. Campbell’s Naval History supplies the maritime context — the transport of the army and the naval operations in the Irish Sea that made William’s campaign possible.
