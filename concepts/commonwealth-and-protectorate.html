The Commonwealth and Protectorate

Summary

The Commonwealth and Protectorate (1649–1660) was the eleven-year republican interregnum between the execution of Charles I and the Restoration of Charles II. It is conventionally divided into two phases: the Commonwealth proper (1649–1653), during which England was governed by the Rump Parliament and a Council of State as a self-proclaimed republic, and the Protectorate (1653–1659), when Oliver Cromwell ruled as Lord Protector under the Instrument of Government. Though brief, this period saw the most radical constitutional experiments in English history — the abolition of monarchy and the House of Lords, the first written constitution, the conquest of Ireland and Scotland, a victorious naval war against the Dutch, and the emergence of England as a major Protestant military power in Europe.

The Rump Parliament and the Commonwealth Declared

After the execution of Charles I on 30 January 1649, the remnant of the House of Commons — reduced by Pride’s Purge (December 1648) to about sixty members, derisively called the “Rump” — moved swiftly to consolidate power. The monarchy was abolished on 17 March 1649; the House of Lords was dissolved on 19 March. On 19 May, England was declared “a Commonwealth and Free State,” governed by the representatives of the people. A Council of State of thirty-eight members, including Cromwell, was appointed to execute the laws and direct military and naval affairs.

The Commonwealth rested on narrow foundations. The Rump controlled perhaps a hundred and fifty members after readmitting some of the excluded and issuing writs for new elections in sympathetic constituencies, but the nation at large was deeply hostile. Royalists, Presbyterians, and the mass of ordinary Englishmen regarded the new regime as a usurpation. Hume records that the Commonwealth found “every thing in England composed into a seeming tranquillity by the terror of their arms” — but it was terror, not consent, that maintained order. An engagement to support the Commonwealth “without king or house of peers” was imposed under severe penalties, yet the people’s reluctance forced the Rump to abandon enforcement.

Cromwell’s Campaigns: Ireland and Scotland

The greatest immediate threats to the Commonwealth were in Ireland and Scotland. In Ireland, the Marquis of Ormond had assembled a royalist army of sixteen thousand men and threatened Dublin. Cromwell was appointed Lord Lieutenant of Ireland in March 1649 and arrived in Dublin on 15 August, after Colonel Michael Jones had already routed Ormond at Rathmines.

Cromwell’s Irish campaign was swift and brutal. At Tredah (Drogheda) in September 1649, he stormed the town after two repulses, and ordered that no quarter be given. The garrison of three thousand men was slaughtered; even those who survived the assault were butchered the next day. At Wexford, the same severity was exercised when English troops rushed in before a capitulation could be completed. Hume writes that Cromwell “pretended to retaliate by this severe execution the cruelty of the Irish massacre: but he well knew that almost the whole garrison was English; and his justice was only a barbarous policy, in order to terrify all other garrisons from resistance.” The policy worked: town after town opened its gates, and within nine months Cromwell had subdued most of Ireland.

In Scotland, the Presbyterians — enraged by the execution of the king and the refusal of the Independents to establish Presbyterian discipline — proclaimed Charles II as king, on condition that he swear to the Covenant. Cromwell, now Captain-General after Fairfax resigned rather than invade Scotland, marched north with sixteen thousand men in July 1650. The Scots, under David Lesley, outnumbered him two to one but were dominated by fanatical clergy who forced Lesley into a rash engagement at Dunbar on 3 September 1650. Cromwell won a decisive victory: three thousand Scots slain, nine thousand taken prisoner. Edinburgh and Leith fell, and the remnant of the Scottish army fled to Stirling.

Charles II, having landed in Scotland and been crowned at Scone (January 1651), led a Scottish army into England in August 1651, hoping that royalist sympathisers would rise. They did not. Cromwell intercepted him at Worcester on 3 September 1651 — exactly one year after Dunbar — and destroyed the royal army. Charles escaped after six weeks of wandering in disguise, famously hiding in an oak tree at Boscobel House, before reaching France. The “crowning mercy,” as Cromwell called Worcester, ended the civil wars.

Naval War and the Dutch Republic

The Commonwealth inherited a formidable navy, and the Rump used it aggressively. The Navigation Act of 1651, which required that goods imported into England be carried in English ships or ships of the country of origin, struck directly at the Dutch carrying trade. Tensions escalated into the First Anglo-Dutch War (1652–1654).

Admiral Robert Blake, who had distinguished himself as a land commander at Taunton and Lyme, proved an admiral of genius. He chased Prince Rupert’s royalist squadron into the Tagus, made prize of twenty Portuguese ships, and threatened Lisbon. In the Channel, Blake, Monk, and Dean fought a series of savage battles against the Dutch admirals Tromp and De Ruyter. The most famous engagement was the battle of 29 July 1653, in which Tromp was shot through the heart. “Though near thirty ships of the Dutch were sunk and taken,” Hume writes, “they little regarded this loss compared with that of their brave admiral.” The war ended with the Treaty of Westminster (April 1654), yielding the honour of the flag to England, an indemnity from the Dutch East India Company, and a secret article excluding the House of Orange from the Stadtholderate.

Expulsion of the Rump and Barebone’s Parliament

By April 1653, the Rump had become increasingly unpopular even within the army. The members, who had sat since 1640, showed no inclination to dissolve themselves and call new elections. On 20 April 1653, Cromwell — acting with a party of musketeers — entered the House and expelled the members by force. “You are no parliament,” he is said to have declared. “I will put an end to your prating.”

Cromwell then summoned a nominated assembly of 128 “godly persons” — five from Scotland, six from Ireland, the rest from England — which met on 4 July 1653. Derisively called “Barebone’s Parliament” after Praise-God Barebone, a London leather-seller and prominent Fifth Monarchist, this body was composed largely of religious radicals: Fifth Monarchists, Anabaptists, Antinomians, and Independents. They set about abolishing tithes, the clerical function, the court of Chancery, and the entire system of English law, intending to replace it with the Mosaic code. Hume describes them as “the very dregs of the fanatics.” Alarmed and embarrassed, Cromwell allowed the moderate members to stage a dissolution on 12 December 1653, restoring authority into his hands.

The Protectorate: Instrument of Government

On 16 December 1653, Cromwell was installed as Lord Protector under the Instrument of Government — England’s first and only written constitution. Drafted by Lambert and the Council of Officers in just four days, the Instrument established a single chief magistrate with extensive but bounded powers. The Protector was supreme magistrate; justice was administered in his name; he had the power of war, peace, and alliance (with council consent); he could pardon all crimes except murder and treason; and he commanded the armed forces jointly with parliament. A standing army of thirty thousand men (twenty thousand foot, ten thousand horse) was established with independent funding. The Protector was obliged to summon a parliament every three years and allow it to sit for five months without dissolution. Bills not receiving the Protector’s assent within twenty days would become law by parliamentary authority alone — the one exception being the army, which could not be reduced without his consent.

The first Protectorate Parliament met on 3 September 1654 — the anniversary Cromwell regarded as fortunate. Though the electoral arrangements were more equitable than the old system (borough-mongering was suppressed, county representation increased, and a 200-pound estate qualification was required for voters), the Commons proved restive. Republicans, Presbyterians, and concealed royalists all opposed the Protectorate. Cromwell was forced to require members to sign a “Recognition” accepting the Instrument before they could sit, and he dissolved the parliament in January 1655 when it showed signs of refusing supply.

Foreign Policy: the Western Design

Cromwell’s foreign policy was a mixture of Protestant crusading zeal and commercial calculation. His “Western Design” — an ambitious plan to seize Spanish colonies in the Caribbean — led to the expedition of 1655 under Admiral Penn and General Venables. The attack on Hispaniola failed disgracefully, but the fleet seized the poorly-defended island of Jamaica in May 1655, which became a permanent English possession.

The war with Spain (1655–1658) was more successful in Europe. Blake blockaded Cadiz and destroyed the Spanish West Indies fleet at Santa Cruz de Tenerife in April 1657. In alliance with France, Cromwell captured Dunkirk from the Spanish in June 1658 — the only continental foothold England held during the century. Blake died in August 1657 off Plymouth, just as his ship entered harbour.

Domestically, Cromwell’s rule grew increasingly authoritarian. After a royalist rising in 1655, he divided England into eleven military districts governed by Major-Generals with sweeping powers. The constitution was replaced in 1657 by the Humble Petition and Advice, which offered Cromwell the crown. After two months of agonised deliberation, he refused — fearing the army’s reaction — but accepted the right to name his successor and the establishment of a second chamber (the Other House).

Richard Cromwell’s Fall and the Restoration

Oliver Cromwell died on 3 September 1658 — his lucky day — and was succeeded by his eldest son, Richard. Richard Cromwell lacked his father’s authority, military backing, or political skill. The army, divided between republicans and officers loyal to the Protectorate, forced Richard to dissolve his parliament and, in May 1659, to abdicate. The Rump Parliament was restored.

The year that followed was one of chaos: the Rump was expelled again by the army, a Committee of Safety was established, General Monk — the commander in Scotland — marched south. Monk, a professional soldier whose motives remain debated, entered London in February 1660, declared for a free parliament, and restored the members secluded by Pride’s Purge. The Long Parliament finally dissolved itself on 16 March 1660, and a Convention Parliament was elected. On 8 May 1660, Charles II was proclaimed king. He landed at Dover on 25 May, and entered London on 29 May — his thirtieth birthday.

Legacy

The Commonwealth and Protectorate left a deep imprint on English political thought. The abolition of monarchy, however brief, demonstrated that kingship could be dispensed with — a precedent that haunted the restored Stuarts and shaped the Revolution of 1688. The constitutional experiments of the Instrument of Government and the Humble Petition and Advice introduced ideas of written constitutions, triennial parliaments, and the separation of powers. Cromwell’s military and naval reforms made England a European power for the first time since the Armada. The Navigation Act and the conquest of Jamaica laid foundations for the British Empire. But the period also established a deep English suspicion of military rule, Puritan austerity, and religious radicalism — a reaction that would characterise the Restoration years.

The term “Interregnum” (Latin for “between reigns”) is the traditional label, but it implicitly denies the regime’s claim to legitimacy. The Commonwealth and Protectorate were, for eleven years, the actual government of England, Scotland, and Ireland — and, for a time, the most formidable military and naval power in Protestant Europe.
