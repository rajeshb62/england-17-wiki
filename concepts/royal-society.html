The Royal Society

Summary

The Royal Society of London for Improving Natural Knowledge, incorporated by Royal Charter in 1662, was the first permanent institution devoted to experimental natural philosophy in the English-speaking world. Conceived during the Civil Wars and Commonwealth at Oxford, formally established in London at the Restoration, and patronised by Charles II, the Society became the institutional engine of the English Scientific Revolution. Its founders — John Wilkins, Robert Boyle, Christopher Wren, William Petty, John Wallis, Seth Ward, and others — were Baconians who rejected scholastic authority in favour of systematic experiment, collaborative observation, and plain descriptive prose. Over the next half-century, under the presidencies of Lord Brouncker, Sir Joseph Williamson, and finally Isaac Newton, the Society transformed English intellectual life and projected its influence across Europe through the Philosophical Transactions, the world’s first exclusively scientific periodical.

Origins: The Oxford Group (c. 1645–1660)

The Royal Society did not spring fully formed from the Restoration court. Its roots were in the private, extra-curial gatherings of natural philosophers that began during the Civil Wars. The most important of these met at Oxford, in the lodgings of John Wilkins at Wadham College — a man whose extraordinary gift for conciliation, Burnet wrote, “made him to be beloved by all parties.” Sprat, himself a Wadham man, marks this as the true birthplace: “Some space after the end of the Civil Wars at Oxford, in Dr. Wilkins his Lodgings, in Wadham College, which was then the place of Resort for Virtuous, and Learned Men, that the first meetings were made, which laid the foundation of all this that followed.”

The Oxford group included the mathematician John Wallis, the astronomer Seth Ward, the physician Thomas Willis, the anatomist Thomas Goddard, the natural philosopher Robert Boyle, the architect and astronomer Christopher Wren, and the political economist William Petty. They met regularly to perform experiments, discuss natural phenomena, and read the works of Francis Bacon, whose New Atlantis and Novum Organum supplied both the ideological programme and the institutional model for what they were attempting. The Civil War and the Interregnum made the university a refuge for learned men who wished to avoid politics and theology; natural philosophy was, as Sprat put it, “Nature alone, which could pleasantly entertain them, in that estate.”

A second group met in London at Gresham College, under the auspices of Samuel Foster, the professor of astronomy. These two circles overlapped and corresponded, and when the Restoration came, the London meetings rapidly eclipsed the Oxford ones.

Foundation at Gresham College (1660)

On 28 November 1660 — twelve weeks after Charles II entered London — twelve men met at Gresham College, in Bishopsgate, after a lecture by Christopher Wren (the professor of astronomy). They resolved to form “a Colledge for the Promoting of Physico-Mathematicall Experimentall Learning.” The minute book records the first list of forty-one names, including the Earl of Brouncker, Robert Boyle, John Wilkins, Christopher Wren, William Petty, and the king’s physician, Sir Thomas Millington. Lord William Brouncker was elected the first President. John Wilkins and Henry Oldenburg were appointed Secretaries. Regular weekly meetings were established, and the Society began immediately to collect instruments, perform experiments, and correspond with foreign philosophers.

The speed of the foundation is itself a Restoration phenomenon. During the Interregnum, the Oxford group had operated informally and discreetly. With the return of monarchy came the possibility of public, legal incorporation. The Society’s founder-members were overwhelmingly royalists or moderate parliamentarians; they had survived the Commonwealth by keeping their heads down, and they presented themselves to the restored King as natural allies of the crown — sober, learned, useful men whose work would strengthen the state.

The Royal Charter (1662)

Charles II granted the Society its first Royal Charter on 15 July 1662, incorporating it as “The Royal Society of London for Improving Natural Knowledge.” The King, who had a genuine if dilettantish interest in chemistry and navigation, expressed his favour in terms that Sprat records: “Your Majesty was the first, who confirm’d this Noble Design of Experiments, by Your own Example, and by a Public Establishment.”

The Charter named Brouncker as President, appointed a Council of twenty-one, and empowered the Society to hold land, elect Fellows, appoint officers, and publish its proceedings. A second Charter in 1663 enlarged the privileges and added the King as Founder and Patron — a role symbolised by the Society’s motto, Nullius in Verba (“On no man’s word”), a declaration of independence from ancient authority and a commitment to experimental evidence as the sole arbiter of truth.

The Charter was more than a legal formality. It gave the Society a permanent corporate identity, independent of the life of any individual patron or sovereign, and it placed the new experimental philosophy under the protection of the English crown — a public endorsement that the French Académie des Sciences (founded 1666) and other continental bodies would later emulate.

Early Leadership and Fellows

Lord William Brouncker (c. 1620–1684), the first President, was a mathematician of genuine ability — he had published the first systematic treatment of continued fractions and served as Chancellor to Queen Catherine. He presided over the Society’s most formative decade, from 1662 to 1677, and was succeeded by Sir Joseph Williamson (1677–1680), the Secretary of State, and then by Sir Christopher Wren (1680–1682).

The early Fellowship was a remarkable concentration of talent. Robert Boyle (1627–1691) was the Society’s most prolific experimentalist — his work with the air pump, published as New Experiments Physico-Mechanical (1660), established the relationship between pressure and volume now known as Boyle’s Law, and his Sceptical Chymist (1661) laid the foundations of modern chemistry. Robert Hooke (1635–1703) was appointed first Curator of Experiments in 1662, charged with preparing three or four experiments for each weekly meeting; his Micrographia (1665) revealed the microscopic world with unprecedented clarity. Christopher Wren contributed anatomical drawings, astronomical observations, and the design of the Society’s meeting rooms. John Wilkins (1614–1672), the Society’s first Secretary, was the intellectual organiser and conciliator whose Essay towards a Real Character and a Philosophical Language (1668) attempted to create a universal language for science. William Petty (1623–1687) brought the quantitative methods of the “political arithmetic” to the Society. Henry Oldenburg (c. 1619–1677), the first Secretary, built the European correspondence network that made the Society the clearing-house of European science — corresponding with Spinoza, Huygens, Leibniz, Malpighi, and dozens of others.

Isaac Newton (1642–1727) was elected a Fellow in 1672, on the strength of his reflecting telescope and his early optical papers. He was an active correspondent of the Society through Oldenburg, though his relationship with the Fellowship was often prickly — his disputes with Hooke over the theory of light and with Leibniz over the calculus soured his temper. He became President in 1703 and was re-elected annually until his death in 1727, presiding over the Society’s most celebrated period.

Burnet records that the Society was also joined by “many Physicians, and other ingenious men,” including Samuel Pepys (President 1684–1686), whose diary records numerous visits to Gresham College to see experiments, and John Evelyn, whose Sylva (1664) on forestry was published under the Society’s auspices.

The Baconian Method and the Programme of Experiments

The Society’s intellectual programme was explicitly Baconian. Francis Bacon had argued in The Advancement of Learning and the Novum Organum that natural philosophy must abandon the syllogism for experiment, that knowledge must be built inductively from particulars, and that science should be a collaborative, institutional enterprise — a vision he had set out fictionally in New Atlantis (1627), with its House of Salomon, the model for the Royal Society itself.

The Society translated this vision into practice. At each weekly meeting, experiments were performed before the assembled Fellows — not as demonstrations of established truths but as genuine investigations. The Curator of Experiments (Hooke) was responsible for designing and executing these trials. Sprat describes the method: “They have a constant succession of experiments, in which every man may take part, either by his hands or his eyes.” The results were recorded, debated, and often repeated publicly to ensure reproducibility — an innovation as important as any single discovery.

The range of early experiments was vast. The air pump, constructed for Boyle by Hooke, generated a vacuum that allowed experiments on respiration, combustion, and the weight of air. Blood transfusion was attempted between dogs in 1665, and a human transfusion — in which Arthur Coga, a “harmless lunatick,” received sheep’s blood — followed in 1667. Microscopic observations by Hooke revealed the cellular structure of cork (coining the word “cell”) and the compound eyes of insects. The Society weighed the earth, tested the strength of gunpowder, observed the transit of Mercury, dissected dozens of animals, and collected natural specimens from every corner of the known world.

The Repository and the Philosophical Transactions

Two institutional innovations of the 1660s proved decisive for the Society’s long-term influence.

The Repository was the Society’s museum of natural and artificial curiosities — specimens, instruments, preserved animals, fossils, minerals, and ethnographic objects. It was housed at Gresham College and later moved with the Society to Crane Court. The Repository served as a physical archive of Baconian natural history, a library of nature against which hypotheses could be tested and from which generalisations could be drawn. Its keeper was Hooke, who curated it alongside his duties as Curator of Experiments.

Far more consequential was the Philosophical Transactions, first published on 6 March 1665 under the editorship of Henry Oldenburg. It was the world’s first periodical devoted exclusively to scientific research. Oldenburg printed letters and papers from correspondents across Europe — often in English or Latin, occasionally in French — and distributed them to subscribers. The Transactions created a public sphere for science. For the first time, a discovery made in London could be read in Paris, Leiden, or Florence within weeks. The publication of Newton’s early optical papers in the Transactions in 1672 — and the ensuing controversy with Hooke — demonstrated both the power and the difficulty of open scientific debate. The Transactions were interrupted by Oldenburg’s death in 1677 but revived by the Society under successive editors; they have been published continuously ever since, making them the oldest scientific journal in the world.

The Great Fire, the Move to Crane Court, and the Eighteenth Century

The Great Fire of London in September 1666 destroyed the Royal Exchange and much of the City, but Gresham College survived — though its neighbourhood was devastated. The Society continued to meet at Gresham until 1710, when the College premises were needed for other uses. In that year, the Society purchased a house at Crane Court, off Fleet Street, which served as its premises until the move to Somerset House in 1780.

The later decades of the seventeenth century saw the Society’s work deepen and specialise. Newton’s Principia Mathematica (1687) — published with the encouragement of the Society and dedicated to it — transformed natural philosophy, establishing universal gravitation and the laws of motion. Newton’s presidency from 1703 marked a shift: the Society became more hierarchical, more mathematical, and more cautious in its experimental programme. Newton himself was a formidable but overbearing President, dominating the Council and directing the Society’s activities with an iron hand. The great days of the 1660s — the air pump, the microscope, the transfusion experiments — receded into institutional memory.

The Society’s Role in the Scientific Revolution

The Royal Society was not the only scientific institution of the seventeenth century — the Académie des Sciences in Paris (1666), the Leopoldina in Germany (1652), and the Accademia del Cimento in Florence (1657) all preceded or paralleled it. But the Society was unique in its combination of features: it was a private corporation, not a state academy; it was open in its membership to gentlemen, physicians, clergymen, and merchants (not merely professional philosophers); it published a regular journal open to external contributors; and it maintained an active experimental programme at its weekly meetings.

The Society’s most important contribution was not any single discovery but the creation of a public, institutional, collaborative model for natural knowledge. Before the Royal Society, science was largely the pursuit of isolated individuals — Kepler, Galileo, Harvey — who published their results privately and corresponded with a small circle. After the Society, science became a profession with a permanent institutional home, a periodical press, a standard of experimental reproducibility, and a rhetoric of public utility. The Society’s motto — Nullius in Verba — captured the epistemological revolution: authority no longer resided in ancient texts, the church, or the prince, but in the disciplined observation of nature by a community of peers.
