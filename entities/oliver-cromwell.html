Oliver Cromwell

Summary

Oliver Cromwell (1599–1658), Lord Protector of England, Scotland, and Ireland from 1653 until his death, was the most extraordinary figure of the English Revolution. A country gentleman turned soldier, a Puritan zealot turned statesman, and a regicide turned sovereign, Cromwell rose from private obscurity to command the New Model Army, overthrow the monarchy, and rule the British Isles as a military dictator cloaked in republican forms. Hume describes him as a man “suited to the age in which he lived, and to that alone” — equally capable of winning the affection of common soldiers by vulgar familiarity and of commanding the obedience of nations by daring enterprise. His legacy remains bitterly contested: to some, he was the champion of parliamentary liberty and Protestant conscience; to others, a hypocritical tyrant whose Irish campaign amounted to genocide and whose rule extinguished the very liberties he had professed to defend.

Early Life and Education

Oliver Cromwell was born at Huntingdon on 25 April 1599, the last year of the sixteenth century, of a good family — the Cromwells had risen under Henry VIII through the dissolution of the monasteries — but his father was a younger son, and Oliver inherited only a small estate. He was sent to Sidney Sussex College, Cambridge, but Hume records that “his genius was found little fitted for the calm and elegant occupations of learning, and he made small proficiencies in his studies.” He left the university on his father’s death in 1617 and appears to have thrown himself into a dissolute course of life: gaming, drinking, and country riots consumed his early years and dissipated part of his patrimony.

All of a sudden, the spirit of reformation seized him. He married Elizabeth Bourchier in 1620, affected a grave and composed behaviour, and entered into all the zeal and rigour of the puritanical party. He offered to restore to everyone whatever sums he had formerly gained by gaming. The same vehemence of temper that had transported him into the extremes of pleasure now distinguished his religious habits. His house became the resort of zealous clergy, and his hospitality to silenced ministers proved as chargeable as his former debaucheries. Though he acquired a tolerable fortune by a maternal uncle, he found his affairs so injured that he was obliged to take a farm at St Ives and apply himself to agriculture. His active mind, superior to the low occupations to which he was condemned, preyed upon itself; he indulged his imagination in visions, illuminations, and revelations — the nourishment of that hypochondriacal temper to which he was ever subject.

He had made a plan with John Hampden, his near kinsman, to transport himself to New England; only an order of council obliged them to disembark and remain in England. His first public opportunity came when he opposed the Earl of Bedford’s drainage of the fens near Ely, revealing the factious zeal and obstinacy that would mark his later career.

Member of Parliament

Cromwell was elected MP for Huntingdon in the Short Parliament (April 1640) and for Cambridge in the Long Parliament (November 1640). In the Commons, he was at first an obscure figure, notable chiefly for his passionate Puritan speeches and his plain, rustic dress. But he attached himself to the war party — those who distrusted any accommodation with the king — and made common cause with Hampden, Pym, and the fiery republicans. Hume notes that Cromwell professed the most exalted sentiments of liberty; he declared to Lord Falkland that “he would not have a drop of blood drawn from the king, but for the security of the laws and liberties of the kingdom.” These professions were accepted at face value by many who would later have reason to reflect on them.

Military Rise: The Ironsides

When war broke out in 1642, Cromwell raised a troop of horse in Cambridgeshire. He proved a natural cavalry commander. His troopers — recruited not from mercenaries or pressed men but from yeoman farmers who shared his Puritan zeal — were subjected to strict discipline and religious instruction. They sang psalms before battle and fought with a conviction that royalist cavalry, for all their gentlemanly courage, could not match. These became the famous Ironsides, the crack cavalry regiment of the parliamentary army.

Cromwell served under the Earl of Manchester in the Eastern Association. At the Battle of Marston Moor (2 July 1644), his disciplined troopers charged and broke Prince Rupert’s cavalry on the royalist right wing. Cromwell himself was wounded in the neck but pressed the pursuit. When he returned to find the parliamentary left wing routed, he rallied his men and, in a second engagement, secured the victory. Marston Moor was the first great parliamentary triumph of the war, and Cromwell’s reputation was made.

He was instrumental in pushing through the Self-Denying Ordinance (1645), which required members of parliament to resign their military commands — but Cromwell was expressly excepted. The New Model Army, a single professional force organised by Fairfax with Cromwell as lieutenant-general, was the instrument that would decide the war.

Naseby and the Decisive Victory

At the Battle of Naseby (14 June 1645), Cromwell commanded the right wing of the New Model Army. While Prince Rupert swept Ireton’s left wing from the field, Cromwell overbore Sir Marmaduke Langdale’s royalist cavalry on the opposite flank. He then turned his horsemen back upon the king’s infantry — a manoeuvre of decisive tactical genius — and threw them into confusion. Only one regiment of royalist foot held its ground; Fairfax broke it in a personal charge, seizing the colours with his own hand. The king’s army was annihilated: five hundred officers and four thousand men taken prisoner, all artillery and ammunition captured, and the king’s cabinet of correspondence seized — the publication of which fatally damaged Charles I’s reputation.

Naseby destroyed the royalist cause. The remaining towns surrendered one by one. Cromwell, now recognised as the soul of the New Model Army, conducted a series of swift sieges that reduced the west of England by the end of 1645.

Political Rise: The Independent Faction

As the war drew to a close, a rift opened between the Presbyterian majority in parliament and the Independent faction in the army. The Presbyterians wanted a negotiated settlement with the king, a Presbyterian church settlement, and the disbandment of the army. The Independents — Cromwell foremost among them — wanted liberty of conscience, security for the army’s arrears, and guarantees against royal revenge. Hume presents Cromwell as the master of this crisis, directing events from behind the scenes while maintaining an appearance of deference to civil authority.

In 1647, the army seized the king’s person from parliamentary custody. Cromwell and his son-in-law Henry Ireton negotiated with Charles — the Heads of the Proposals — demanding a constitutional monarchy, biennial parliaments, and toleration. But Charles, believing he could play the Scots, the Presbyterians, and the army off against each other, prevaricated. When the king escaped to the Isle of Wight and signed the Engagement with the Scots, a second civil war became inevitable.

Pride’s Purge and the King’s Trial

Cromwell crushed the royalist insurgency with devastating efficiency. At Preston (17 August 1648), he attacked the Scots under Hamilton and Langdale with only eight thousand men against twenty thousand. He defeated Langdale’s corps, routed Hamilton, and pursued him to Utoxeter, where the Scottish commander surrendered. Marching into Scotland, Cromwell joined Argyle and placed power in the hands of the extreme Presbyterian party.

By the time he returned to London, the army had resolved to bring the king to justice. On 6 December 1648, Colonel Pride forcibly excluded some 140 Presbyterian MPs from the House of Commons — Pride’s Purge — leaving only the Rump of about sixty members who would do the army’s bidding. Hume leaves no doubt that Cromwell, though not physically present in London, was the architect of this coup.

Cromwell was among the commissioners of the High Court of Justice that tried the king. He signed the death warrant for the execution of Charles I on 30 January 1649 — an act he later insisted was “a necessary work” and “a thing of God’s own bringing to pass.” Hume recounts a story that Cromwell, after the execution, visited the king’s body and was heard to murmur, “Cruel necessity.” The regicide made Cromwell a pariah in the eyes of every crowned head in Europe and bound him irrevocably to the survival of the republic.

Conquest of Ireland: Drogheda and Wexford

In August 1649, Cromwell was dispatched to Ireland to suppress the coalition of Irish Catholics and Royalists under the Marquess of Ormond. He landed at Dublin with an experienced army and immediately marched on Drogheda (Tredah). The town was well fortified with a garrison of three thousand under Sir Arthur Aston. Cromwell, knowing the importance of speed, assaulted the breach after only a short siege. Though twice repulsed, he renewed the attack with Ireton at his side and carried the town by storm. Orders were given to give no quarter. “Cromwell pretended to retaliate by this severe execution the cruelty of the Irish massacre,” writes Hume, “but he well knew that almost the whole garrison was English; and his justice was only a barbarous policy, in order to terrify all other garrisons from resistance.”

At Wexford, a similar massacre followed when the town fell prematurely. The policy worked: town after town surrendered without resistance. By the time Cromwell was recalled to England in May 1650, Ireland was broken. He left the government to Ireton, who continued the work of subduing and dispossessing the Catholic population under the Act of Settlement. The memory of Drogheda has made Cromwell a figure of execration in Irish history for centuries.

Conquest of Scotland: Dunbar and Worcester

With Ireland subdued, Cromwell was summoned to face the greater danger: the Scots had crowned Charles II and were arming for an invasion of England. Fairfax, scrupling to invade a Presbyterian ally, resigned the command. Cromwell succeeded him as captain-general of all the forces in England — the decisive step towards sovereign power.

Cromwell invaded Scotland with sixteen thousand men. The Scottish commander, David Lesley, adopted a Fabian strategy, refusing battle and entrenching in fortified positions. Disease and supply shortages reduced the English army. But on 3 September 1650, Cromwell caught Lesley at a disadvantage near Dunbar, attacked, and won a stunning victory. About three thousand Scots were killed and ten thousand taken prisoner. Cromwell wrote that “the Lord hath done great things for us.”

When Charles II marched into England with a Scottish army the following year, Cromwell pursued and trapped him at Worcester. On 3 September 1651 — the first anniversary of Dunbar — he attacked the city from three sides and annihilated the royalist army. Charles escaped after six weeks of fugitive wandering, famously hiding in an oak tree at Boscobel. Worcester was Cromwell’s “crowning mercy” — the last battle of the civil wars.

The Lord Protector

Military victory presented a political problem. The Rump Parliament had governed since the king’s execution but had lost all public credit, devoting itself to self-perpetuation and private interest. On 20 April 1653, Cromwell entered the chamber in anger, denounced the members as drunkards and whoremasters, and — with a troop of soldiers — expelled them. “The Lord has done with you,” he shouted. “You are no parliament.”

The Barebone’s Parliament (July–December 1653), an assembly of 140 Puritan “saints” nominated by the army, proved unworkable. The moderate members resigned their power into Cromwell’s hands, and the Instrument of Government — the first written constitution in English history — was adopted. It vested executive authority in a single person as Lord Protector, with a council of state and a triennial parliament.

Cromwell was installed as Lord Protector on 16 December 1653. He refused the title of king on three occasions — most famously in 1657 when the Humble Petition and Advice offered him the crown — but his authority was monarchical in all but name. He lived at Whitehall, maintained a court, appointed peers, and commanded the armed forces. Hume observes that he was sincere in refusing the crown; he knew the army, which had been taught that kingship was a usurpation upon Christ, would never accept it.

Puritan Rule and Domestic Administration

Cromwell’s domestic administration displayed, in Hume’s judgment, “as great regard both to justice and clemency as his usurped authority, derived from no law, and founded only on the sword, could possibly permit.” The judges were men of integrity; the law was, for ordinary subjects, the great rule of conduct. He divided the country into eleven military districts under major-generals, charged with suppressing royalist conspiracies and enforcing Puritan morality. Stage plays were banned; horse racing, bear-baiting, and cock-fighting were suppressed; the observance of the Sabbath was strictly enforced.

He established liberty of conscience for all Protestant denominations except Anglicans and, for a time, Catholics. The Church of England was replaced by a system of “tryers” — commissioners who examined candidates for the ministry not on their learning but on their progress in grace. Cromwell was familiar and easy with the “saints” of all denominations, talking spiritually with them, weeping, sighing, and praying, while always retaining political control. “I am the only man,” he was heard to say, “who has known how to subdue that insolent sect [the Presbyterians], which can suffer none but itself.”

Foreign Policy: The Western Design

Cromwell’s foreign policy was ambitious, aggressive, and confessional. He saw England as a Protestant power destined to challenge the Catholic Habsburg hegemony. His first major initiative was peace with the Dutch Republic (April 1654), ending the first Anglo-Dutch War (1652–1654) on terms favourable to England, including the Navigation Act that excluded Dutch shipping from English trade.

He then launched the Western Design against Spain’s American empire. An expedition under Admiral Penn and General Venables sailed for the West Indies in 1654 and attacked Hispaniola in April 1655 — a disastrous failure. The English troops were poorly led and the Spanish defences were stronger than expected. As consolation, the expedition seized Jamaica, which surrendered without serious resistance. Jamaica proved a valuable acquisition, though Hume calls the whole scheme “imprudent and impolitic.”

The war with Spain (1655–1659) was Cromwell’s most characteristic undertaking: a Protestant crusade conducted with English sea power. Admiral Blake destroyed the Spanish treasure fleet at Santa Cruz de Tenerife in April 1657 — “the last and greatest action of the gallant Blake.” Cromwell allied with France against Spain, and in 1658, English troops under Turenne distinguished themselves at the Battle of the Dunes, capturing Dunkirk, which was delivered to English possession. France’s Cardinal Mazarin called Cromwell “a fortunate madman.”

The protector’s foreign policy, for all its recklessness, restored English prestige abroad. Hume concedes that “the great mind of this successful usurper was intent on spreading the renown of the English nation” and that he made the name of Englishman as feared and revered as that of a Roman.

Death

Cromwell’s last years were tormented. His health declined under the weight of public cares; he was haunted by the fear of assassination, wearing armour under his clothes and carrying pistols; never sleeping three nights in the same chamber; and never moving without strong guards. His daughter Elizabeth Claypole, his favourite child, lay dying and urged him to repent of his bloody measures. His son-in-law Fleetwood reproached him for personal ambition. The royalists and the republicans conspired; the army was mutinous.

A slow fever seized him, changing into a tertian ague. His chaplains assured him of recovery by divine revelation, and he dismissed the physicians: “I am well assured of my recovery. It is promised by the Lord.” But the symptoms worsened. On 3 September 1658 — his fortunate day, the anniversary of Dunbar and Worcester — he expired. A violent tempest burst in the wake of his death. He was buried with more than royal pomp in Westminster Abbey, only to be exhumed at the Restoration, hanged in his shroud at Tyburn, and beheaded: his head displayed on a pole outside Westminster Hall for a quarter-century.

Character and Legacy

Cromwell’s character, Hume writes, has been painted by his admirers as extravagant panegyric and by his enemies as virulent invective — and both are supported by striking circumstances. He was a man of boundless courage and tactical genius, the finest cavalry commander of his age. He was also a consummate dissembler, able to weep on demand and to cloak the most cold-blooded policy in the language of divine inspiration. His Puritan piety was both genuine and instrumental; the same emotional intensity that drove his religious ecstasies also drove his most ruthless decisions.

His military achievements were extraordinary: he rose from obscurity to command the most formidable army in Europe, conquered Ireland, subdued Scotland, and destroyed the royalist cause in England. His foreign policy made England a power to be reckoned with. His domestic rule was efficient, just in ordinary cases, and remarkable for its religious toleration — a rare virtue in an age of persecution.

But the means by which he attained power were violent and lawless: the purging of parliament, the execution of the king, the massacres in Ireland, the military dictatorship. He left no constitutional settlement that outlasted him. Within two years of his death, the monarchy was restored and his corpse was dragged from its grave.

Cromwell was, in the last analysis, a man of the crisis: a revolutionary who could break but could not build, a soldier who could conquer but could not legitimise, a Puritan who could pray but could not forgive. His body was no sooner in the grave than the edifice of his power began to crumble. Richard Cromwell, his son and designated successor, meekly resigned the protectorship. The republic collapsed. And the nation, weary of saints and soldiers, called back the king.
