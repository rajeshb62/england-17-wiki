John Churchill, 1st Duke of Marlborough

Summary

John Churchill (1650–1722), 1st Duke of Marlborough, was the greatest English general of his age and one of the foremost military commanders in European history. His career spanned the reigns of five monarchs — Charles II, James II, William III, Anne, and George I — and his four great victories in the War of the Spanish Succession (Blenheim, Ramillies, Oudenarde, and Malplaquet) shattered the military supremacy of Louis XIV and established England as a first-rate continental power. Yet his character was as complex as his achievements were brilliant: a man of graceful bearing and unfailing courtesy, he was also acquisitive, secretive, and capable of a cold ingratitude that shocked even his contemporaries. Burnet judged him “one of the greatest men the age has produced” while acknowledging that his hunger for fortune was never fully shaken off, even in his highest elevation. His 17th-century career — service under Turenne, the Tangier garrison, the victory at Sedgemoor, the defection to William of Orange, the Irish campaign, and the long years of disgrace — was the foundation on which his later fame was built.

Early Career under Charles II and James II

John Churchill was born about 1650 into a family of minor Devonshire gentry ruined by the Civil War. His father, Sir Winston Churchill, was a Cavalier who had fought for Charles I and suffered sequestration. The young Churchill was placed as a page in the household of the Duke of York (the future James II) and began his military education in earnest by serving as a volunteer in the French army under the great Marshal Turenne. Hume records that during the campaign of 1675, at the Battle of Consarbruck and the retreat after Turenne’s death, “the Duke of Marlborough, then captain Churchill, here learned the rudiments of that art which he afterward practised with such fatal success against France.” He distinguished himself by his coolness under fire and was promoted by Turenne himself — an early mark of the military genius that would later humble France.

Returning to England, Churchill was given a commission in the English forces sent to garrison Tangier, the Moroccan possession acquired through Charles II’s marriage to Catherine of Braganza. The Tangier service — among the veterans who later became known as the “Tangerines” — was a hard school: the garrison fought constant skirmishes with Moorish forces in a pestilential climate. Churchill’s conduct there brought him to the notice of the Duke of York, who advanced him through a succession of commands. By 1683 he was a colonel and had been raised to the Scottish peerage as Lord Churchill of Eyemouth.

When Charles II died in February 1685 and James II succeeded, Churchill was one of the few men in whom the new king reposed entire confidence. James had known him since boyhood and had promoted him steadily; Churchill had repaid that confidence with conspicuous loyalty during the succession crisis. When the Duke of Monmouth landed at Lyme Regis in June 1685 to claim the crown, Churchill was sent as second-in-command to the Earl of Feversham to suppress the rebellion. At the Battle of Sedgemoor (5 July 1685), the rebel infantry attacked the royal camp by night and threw the veteran forces into disorder; but Churchill’s steady handling of the cavalry, combined with Monmouth’s own indecision and Lord Gray’s cowardice, turned the tide. After three hours the rebels gave way, and some fifteen hundred were slaughtered in the pursuit. Churchill emerged from the campaign with his reputation enhanced, though the savagery of the Bloody Assizes that followed was the work of Kirke and Jefferies, not of the young general.

Defection to William of Orange

The contradiction at the heart of Churchill’s character — his capacity for profound ingratitude to a master who had raised him from nothing — was revealed in the crisis of 1688. James II’s campaign to Catholicise the army and the state had driven even his most loyal servants to despair. Churchill, according to Burnet, had told the Earl of Gallway as early as James’s accession that “if the King was ever prevailed on to alter our religion, he would serve him no longer, but withdraw from him.” He was no party to the King’s violent measures, and he was profoundly doubtful of the legitimacy of the Prince of Wales born in June 1688 — the so-called “warming-pan baby” that the Whigs insisted was a supposititious child smuggled into the Queen’s bed.

Churchill quietly committed himself to the Prince of Orange’s enterprise. He undertook that Prince George of Denmark and the Princess Anne would leave the Court and join the Prince as soon as possible. When William landed at Torbay in November 1688, Churchill was with the royal army at Salisbury. On the night of 24 November, he rode out of the camp at Axminster with the Duke of Grafton and Colonel Berkeley, taking several troops of dragoons with him, and joined the Prince. Hume writes that Churchill “had been raised from the rank of a page, had been invested with a high command in the army, had been created a peer, and had owed his whole fortune to the king’s favour: yet even he could resolve, during the present extremity, to desert his unhappy master, who had ever reposed entire confidence in him.” The blow broke James’s spirit. His daughter Anne, guided by Lady Churchill, fled London that same night, and within days the King had lost all heart for resistance and was in flight for France.

Service under William III: The Irish Campaign

William III, though he could never entirely trust a man who had deserted his former master, recognised Churchill’s military talents. In 1689 Churchill was created Earl of Marlborough and sent to Flanders to command the English contingent of the Grand Alliance. His first independent command of note came in 1690, when William, then engaged in Ireland, authorised Marlborough to undertake an amphibious expedition against the southern Irish ports held by the Jacobites.

Marlborough proposed to strike at Cork and Kinsale, which commanded the communications between France and the Irish Jacobites. Contrary winds delayed his departure until October, a dangerously late season for campaigning. He took Cork by storm with impressive speed: four thousand prisoners fell into his hands, though the assault cost the life of the Duke of Grafton, who received a mortal wound. From Cork he marched to Kinsale, where he found the two forts that commanded the port far stronger than the intelligence reports had indicated. Burnet records that Marlborough told him “if he had known their true strength, he had never undertaken the expedition, in a season so far advanced.” Yet within a few days the place capitulated. The reduction of these two ports cut off the quick communication between France and Ireland and contributed materially to the eventual Jacobite defeat at Limerick. It was a model campaign — bold in conception, rapid in execution, and economical in blood.

Disgrace and Imprisonment

Despite his Irish success, Marlborough’s position under William was precarious. The King suspected him — not without reason — of maintaining secret correspondence with the exiled James II at St. Germains. In January 1692, William dismissed Marlborough from all his employments. Burnet notes that Admiral Russell “almost upbraided the King with the Earl of Marlborough’s Services, who, as he said, had set the Crown on his head.” Russell’s intercession availed nothing. Worse followed: in May 1692, as a French invasion fleet prepared to land James II in England, Marlborough was sent to the Tower of London on a charge of high treason. The accusation, Dalrymple records, was “a forged accusation” by profligate creatures who “fancied that forgeries and false swearing would be as acceptable, and as well rewarded, in this Reign, as they had been formerly.” The conspiracy was eventually detected and the forgers whipped and pilloried, but Marlborough was held in the Tower for several weeks and released only on bail.

The causes of his disgrace were complex. His wife Sarah’s intimacy with Princess Anne had made the Marlboroughs objects of Queen Mary’s jealousy. And there was some truth to the rumours of Jacobite correspondence: Marlborough, ever the trimmer, appears to have maintained a line of communication with St. Germains as insurance against a Stuart restoration. Yet Dalrymple suggests that King William himself may have tacitly authorised such contacts as a channel of intelligence. At a later period, William restored Marlborough to his rank and employed him in great services — partly from “that indulgence for recent prejudices, to which he yielded more than most of his subjects, and partly because he found his business done better by that Lord than by any other person.” Marlborough was made governor to the young Duke of Gloucester, Princess Anne’s sole surviving child, and was gradually readmitted to military employment in the closing years of William’s reign.

Rise under Queen Anne

The death of William III in March 1702 and the accession of Queen Anne transformed Marlborough’s fortunes. Anne had been the intimate friend of his wife Sarah Churchill since childhood, and the queen’s affection for “Mrs. Freeman” — the pet name by which Sarah was known in their correspondence — gave the Marlboroughs an ascendancy at Court such as no subject had enjoyed since Buckingham. Within a week of her accession, Anne sent Marlborough to Holland to assure the Dutch States-General of her commitment to the war against France. He was appointed Captain-General of the English forces, and the States gave him effective command of the allied army. In December 1702, after a successful first campaign that saw the capture of Venlo, Ruremond, Stevenswaert, and Liege, Anne raised him to a dukedom — the Duke of Marlborough — and both Houses of Parliament sent their formal thanks.

The years that followed were the summit of Marlborough’s career and of England’s military glory. His strategy was founded on two principles: the destruction of the Franco-Bavarian alliance that threatened Vienna, and the bold offensive that the cautious Dutch generals were always reluctant to authorise. In 1703 he took Bonn and Huy; in 1704 he conceived a design of breathtaking audacity.

Blenheim (1704)

In the spring of 1704, Vienna was in mortal peril. The Elector of Bavaria had joined Louis XIV, and a combined Franco-Bavarian army under Marshal Tallard and the Elector threatened to take the Imperial capital. Marlborough resolved on a march that contemporaries regarded as madness: he would move the allied army from the Low Countries to the Danube, a distance of some 250 miles, across the lines of communication of the enemy, without losing a man to desertion or enemy action. He accomplished this feat with such secrecy and discipline that the French had no idea of his true destination until he was already in Germany. At Schellenberg he stormed the heights overlooking Donauwörth in a ferocious assault that cleared the passage to Bavaria.

On 13 August 1704, Marlborough and Prince Eugene of Savoy — the imperial general who became his close collaborator — faced the combined Franco-Bavarian army at the village of Blenheim on the Danube. The French were strongly posted, with the Danube on one flank and a marshy rivulet in front. Marlborough and Eugene attacked nonetheless. Burnet, who received Marlborough’s own account of the battle, writes that the Duke told him “he never saw more evident Characters of a special Providence” than that day. A cannonball struck the ground so near him that he was “some time quite covered with the Cloud of Dust and Earth that it raised about him.” The battle was decided by Marlborough’s cavalry, which broke through the French centre after the infantry had pinned the enemy in place. Tallard was taken prisoner, and twelve thousand French infantry — trapped in the village of Blenheim — laid down their arms. The French and Bavarians lost some forty thousand men killed, wounded, and taken; the allies about twelve thousand. Vienna was saved, Bavaria was conquered, and the myth of French invincibility was shattered. The Emperor created Marlborough a Prince of the Empire, with the principality of Mindelheim assigned to him.

Ramillies, Oudenarde, and Malplaquet

The victories continued. At Ramillies (23 May 1706), Marlborough destroyed a French army under Marshal Villeroy in what many military historians consider his most perfect battle. He feinted at the French left, drew Villeroy into reinforcing it, then struck the weakened centre with overwhelming force. The French army disintegrated; Marlborough captured almost the whole of the Spanish Netherlands — including Brussels, Ghent, Bruges, Antwerp, and Ostend — in a single whirlwind campaign. The French lost some fifteen thousand men; the allies fewer than four thousand.

At Oudenarde (11 July 1708), Marlborough and Eugene caught a French army under the Duke of Vendome and the Duke of Burgundy in the midst of crossing the Scheldt and attacked before the enemy could deploy. The battle was a confused, six-hour melee in which Marlborough’s tactical genius and his power of personal command on the field turned a potential stand-off into a decisive victory. The French were driven back into Ghent in disorder.

The bloodiest of Marlborough’s victories came at Malplaquet (11 September 1709). The French, now commanded by Marshal Villars, had fortified their position with elaborate fieldworks — a foretaste of the entrenchments that would dominate eighteenth-century warfare. Marlborough and Eugene, overruling Dutch caution, attacked the entrenchments in a series of frontal assaults. The slaughter was terrible: the allies lost some twenty-four thousand killed and wounded to the French fifteen thousand. Yet the French army retreated in good order, and Villars declared that he had lost the field, not the honour. Malplaquet was the most pyrrhic of Marlborough’s victories, and it marked the beginning of the war-weariness that would eventually undo him.

Fall from Power, Exile, and Return

Marlborough’s political position rested on the favour of Queen Anne and the partnership of Lord Godolphin, the Lord Treasurer, in what the Tories called the “duumvirate.” But the war had become increasingly unpopular as its costs mounted. The Battle of Malplaquet, with its dreadful casualty list, and the failure to achieve a decisive peace weakened Marlborough’s hold on the public. His wife Sarah, once the Queen’s dearest friend, had become overbearing and quarrelsome; Anne had transferred her affections to a new favourite, Abigail Masham, a cousin of Robert Harley, who led the Tory opposition.

In 1710, the Tories swept to power in a general election. Harley became Chancellor of the Exchequer and effectively prime minister. In January 1711, Anne, under pressure from Harley and the Tories, dismissed Sarah from her posts at Court. Marlborough himself was accused of peculation — of having taken bribes from the army contractor Medina and of having enriched himself at public expense. The charges were genuine enough in their substance if inflated in their presentation; Marlborough, ever acquisitive, had indeed taken a percentage on the bread and transport contracts of the allied army.

In December 1711, Anne dismissed Marlborough from all his employments. The Captain-General who had saved Europe from French domination was stripped of his commands, and the Duke of Ormond was sent to replace him in Flanders. Marlborough went into voluntary exile on the Continent, where he remained until the death of Queen Anne in August 1714. The accession of George I restored him to favour: he was reappointed Captain-General and given command of the army, but his health was broken and he took no further active part in military affairs. He suffered a series of strokes, and died at Windsor on 16 June 1722.

Character and Legacy

Burnet’s character of Churchill, written before his greatest battles, remains the most penetrating portrait. He was “a man of a noble and graceful appearance, bred up in the Court with no literature: But he had a solid and clear understanding, with a constant presence of mind. He knew the arts of living in a Court beyond any man in it. He caressed all people with a soft and obliging deportment, and was always ready to do good offices. He had no fortune to set up on. This put him on all the methods of acquiring one. And that went so far into him, that he did not shake it off, when he was in a much higher elevation.”

The 17th-century foundation of his career — the years as a page in James’s household, the service under Turenne, the Tangier garrison, the victory at Sedgemoor, the defection to William, the Irish campaign, the Tower — was essential to everything that followed. It forged the soldier who would never lose a battle, the courtier who could navigate the treacherous waters of Stuart and Hanoverian politics, and the statesman who, for a decade, was the most powerful man in England after the Queen. He was laid to rest in Westminster Abbey, and his memory is preserved in Blenheim Palace, the vast Oxfordshire house that a grateful Queen Anne had begun to build for him before their friendship soured — a monument in stone to the greatest captain of his age.
