Charles I of England

Summary

Charles I (r. 1625–1649), the second Stuart monarch of England, is one of the most consequential and tragic figures in English history. His reign was defined by an escalating conflict between royal prerogative and parliamentary authority, culminating in the English Civil War (1642–1649), his defeat, trial, and public execution on 30 January 1649. He was the first English sovereign to be tried and executed by his own subjects — an event that shook monarchies across Europe and established, for a time, a republican commonwealth in England.

Charles was a man of high personal virtue — a devoted husband, a patron of the arts, a sincere Anglican, and a brave soldier — but his political inflexibility, his reliance on controversial ministers (Buckingham, Strafford, Laud), and his willingness to govern without parliament for eleven years proved fatal. The Caroline era (from Carolus, Latin for Charles) saw the flowering of English portrait painting (Van Dyck) and architecture (Inigo Jones), but also the collapse of the Stuart monarchy into civil war and regicide.

Accession and the First Parliaments (1625–1626)

Charles succeeded his father James I on 27 March 1625. He was twenty-four years old, inexperienced, and deeply influenced by George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham — the favourite who had dominated the latter part of James’s reign and whom Charles regarded not merely as a minister but as a friend. Within weeks of his accession, Charles summoned his first parliament, eager to prove his trust in the Commons and to secure the supply necessary for the war with Spain that the previous parliament had demanded.

The Commons responded by granting a meagre two subsidies (about 112,000 pounds) — far below the king’s needs. Hume records that Charles, expecting the dutiful generosity of his subjects, was cruelly disappointed. The parliament was swiftly adjourned to Oxford to escape plague, but the Commons there grew more hostile, turning their attack upon Buckingham, whose conduct of affairs they deemed ruinous. When the Commons hesitated to grant supply, Charles dissolved the first parliament (August 1625) without having obtained the funds he required.

A second parliament met in February 1626. The Commons immediately moved to impeach Buckingham for corruption and mismanagement. Charles, who regarded any attack on Buckingham as an attack on his own authority, responded by imprisoning two members — Sir Dudley Digges and Sir John Eliot — who had spoken against the favourite. The Lords refused to proceed with impeachment, but the Commons would not grant supply. Charles dissolved the second parliament in June 1626, having received nothing but grievances.

The Cadiz Expedition and the Isle of Rhé

Meanwhile, Charles had committed English arms to two disastrous expeditions, both planned under Buckingham’s direction. The first, an attack on Cadiz (October 1625), was intended to intercept the Spanish treasure fleet but was mismanaged from the start. The troops landed, became drunk on captured wine, and were re-embarked in disorder. The expedition returned home in humiliation.

The second and more ruinous venture was the expedition to the Isle of Rhé (July–November 1627), intended to relieve the Huguenot stronghold of La Rochelle, then besieged by Cardinal Richelieu. Buckingham personally commanded the landing force, but the siege of the citadel of St Martin was incompetently conducted, the French received reinforcements, and the English were forced to retreat with heavy losses. Hume writes that scarce an illustrious family but mourned the loss of a son or brother. The disaster discredited both Buckingham and the king’s foreign policy.

The Petition of Right and Buckingham’s Assassination (1628)

By 1628, Charles was desperate for supply. Forced loans, arbitrary imprisonment, and the billeting of soldiers had inflamed the nation. Preparations for a third parliament were made with trepidation on both sides.

The third parliament met in March 1628. The Commons, led by Sir Edward Coke, Sir John Eliot, and Sir Thomas Wentworth (the future Earl of Strafford), resolved to secure the liberties of the subject before granting supply. They framed the Petition of Right, a landmark constitutional document that declared:

  • No forced loans or taxes without parliamentary consent
  • No imprisonment without cause shown (habeas corpus)
  • No billeting of soldiers in private houses
  • No martial law in peacetime

Charles, needing money to continue the war, gave a reluctant and ambiguous royal assent (7 June 1628). The Commons, satisfied, voted five subsidies. But Charles never fully accepted the Petition of Right as a binding limitation on his prerogative, and the ambiguity of his assent poisoned trust between crown and parliament for the remainder of his reign.

On 23 August 1628, Buckingham was stabbed to death in Portsmouth by John Felton, a disgruntled army officer who had been passed over for promotion. Felton, who had served in the disastrous Rhé expedition and nursed a grievance at being denied a captaincy, struck the duke with a knife as he passed through a crowded room. The assassination was greeted with public rejoicing. Charles, however, was deeply grieved; he wore mourning for his friend and insisted that Felton be put to torture (the judges refused, as it was contrary to law). With Buckingham’s death, Charles became his own minister and never again reposed such unlimited confidence in any single subject.

The Personal Rule (1629–1640)

The third parliament was dissolved in March 1629 after a tumultuous session in which the Speaker was held down in his chair while the Commons passed resolutions against Arminianism and the levying of tonnage and poundage without consent. Charles, disgusted with parliaments, resolved to govern without them. There would be no parliament for eleven years — a period often called the Personal Rule or the Eleven Years’ Tyranny.

Finance and Ship Money

Without parliamentary supply, Charles raised revenue through a variety of expedients. Ancient feudal dues were revived, monopolies were sold, forest boundaries were extended, and fines were imposed on landowners who had encroached on royal forests. The most controversial measure was Ship Money — an ancient tax on maritime counties for the defence of the coast, which Charles in 1635 extended to inland counties. The writs claimed that the king, as guardian of the realm, could levy this tax in times of danger without parliamentary consent.

John Hampden, a Buckinghamshire gentleman, refused to pay the 20-shilling assessment. His case was tried before the Court of Exchequer in 1637–1638. The judges, by a narrow majority of seven to five, found for the crown. But the trial became a national cause célèbre, and the narrowness of the verdict was seen as a moral victory for the opposition. Hampden was celebrated as a patriot, and the Crown’s legal position, though technically upheld, was fatally weakened in public opinion.

Laud and Religious Innovation

William Laud, Archbishop of Canterbury from 1633, was the dominant ecclesiastical figure of the Personal Rule. A high-church Arminian, Laud sought to impose uniformity in worship — restoring the communion table to the east end of churches, railing it in, and calling it an altar; requiring bowing at the name of Jesus; and enforcing the wearing of surplices and copes. These innovations, which Hume describes as reviving the superstitions of the fourth and fifth centuries, were deeply offensive to the Puritan majority. Many Englishmen suspected that Laud aimed to lead the Church of England back to Rome.

The enforcement of Laud’s reforms through the Court of High Commission and the Star Chamber was severe. Three Puritan pamphleteers — William Prynne, Henry Burton, and John Bastwick — were pilloried, branded, and had their ears cropped for seditious libel. The cruelty of their punishment excited widespread sympathy and further discredited the regime.

Strafford in Ireland

Thomas Wentworth, created Earl of Strafford in 1640, governed Ireland from 1633 with a ruthless efficiency that made him the most capable — and most feared — of Charles’s ministers. He increased the Irish revenue, built up a standing army (which Charles hoped to use for his own purposes), and enforced order with severity. Strafford’s policy was “Thorough” — an unflinching assertion of royal authority against all opposition. He made enemies on all sides, and his enemies in England would destroy him at the first opportunity.

The Bishops’ Wars and the Collapse of Personal Rule (1637–1640)

The trigger for the collapse of Charles’s Personal Rule came not from England but from Scotland. In 1637, Charles and Laud attempted to impose a new Prayer Book — based on the English Book of Common Prayer — on the Scottish Kirk. When the Dean of Edinburgh attempted to read the new liturgy at St Giles’ Cathedral on 23 July 1637, a riot erupted. A market-woman named Jenny Geddes is said to have thrown her stool at the Dean, crying “Darest thou say mass in my lug?”

The Scots responded by organising a National Covenant (February 1638) — a mass petition pledging resistance to the religious innovations. When Charles attempted to enforce his authority, the Scots raised an army under Alexander Leslie, veterans of the Thirty Years’ War.

The First Bishops’ War (1639) was a standoff: Charles assembled an English army near Berwick, but his troops were poorly equipped and reluctant to fight their northern neighbours. A truce was signed without a battle.

The Second Bishops’ War (1640) was a disaster. The Scots invaded England, routed a hastily assembled English force at Newburn (August 1640), and occupied Newcastle. Charles, humiliated and without money, was forced to call first the Short Parliament (April–May 1640), which refused supply and was dissolved after three weeks, and then the Long Parliament.

The Long Parliament (1640–1642)

The Long Parliament met in November 1640 and, in what Hume calls “the most memorable parliament of English history,” proceeded to dismantle the apparatus of Personal Rule. Led by John Pym, the parliamentary opposition:

  • Impeached Strafford, who was tried for high treason, attainted by bill, and executed on Tower Hill (12 May 1641) — Charles, after agonised hesitation, signed the death warrant, a decision that haunted him for the rest of his life
  • Impeached Laud, who was imprisoned (executed in 1645)
  • Abolished the Star Chamber and the Court of High Commission (July 1641)
  • Passed the Triennial Act, requiring parliament to be summoned at least every three years
  • Forced Charles to dismiss his unpopular ministers

In November 1641, the Commons passed the Grand Remonstrance — a comprehensive catalogue of grievances against Charles’s government, passed by a narrow majority of 159 to 148. The king’s response was disastrous. In January 1642, he marched into the House of Commons with several hundred armed men to arrest Pym, Hampden, and three other opposition leaders — the Five Members. They had been warned and had fled. The breach of parliamentary privilege was irreparable. Charles left London, which was now in open opposition to him, and established his court at York.

The Civil War (1642–1649)

The First Campaign

On 22 August 1642, Charles raised his standard at Nottingham, formally committing himself to war. The nation divided: roughly half supported the king (Cavaliers), half supported parliament (Roundheads). London, the eastern counties, and the navy supported parliament; the north and west supported the king. Both sides expected a short war.

The first major engagement was the Battle of Edgehill (23 October 1642), a confused and inconclusive fight in which neither side gained a decisive advantage. Prince Rupert of the Rhine, Charles’s dashing nephew, led the royalist cavalry to initial success but failed to press the advantage. Both armies settled into a winter of manoeuvre and negotiation.

The war swung back and forth through 1643. The royalists won a series of victories in the west — at Stratton, Lansdown, and Roundway Down — and captured Bristol, the second city of the kingdom. But the parliamentarian cause was saved by the political and military skill of John Hampden (mortally wounded at Chalgrove Field, June 1643), and by the alliance with Scotland: parliament agreed to the Solemn League and Covenant with the Scottish Covenanters, who sent an army into England in exchange for the promise of Presbyterian uniformity.

Marston Moor (1644)

The most important battle of the first civil war was Marston Moor (2 July 1644), near York. A combined parliamentarian and Scottish army, including Oliver Cromwell’s Ironsides cavalry, routed the royalist army under Prince Rupert. Yorkshire was lost to the king. The battle was a turning point: the north of England fell under parliamentarian control, and the reputation of Cromwell and his disciplined cavalry was established.

Naseby (1645)

Parliament reorganised its forces into the New Model Army under Sir Thomas Fairfax and Cromwell, with the Self-Denying Ordinance removing members of parliament (except Cromwell) from military commands. At Naseby (14 June 1645), the New Model Army destroyed the main royalist army. The king’s private papers were captured, revealing his negotiations with the Irish Catholics and his determination not to yield to parliament — disclosures that fatally damaged his reputation.

Thereafter, the royalist cause collapsed. Fairfax swept through the west, capturing Bristol and routing the remaining royalist forces. Charles, his army gone, surrendered to the Scots at Newark (May 1646). The Scots, after protracted negotiation, handed him over to the English parliament in January 1647.

Captivity, Negotiations, and the Second Civil War

Charles was held at Holdenby House while parliamentary factions — Presbyterians and Independents — debated the future settlement. Charles, with characteristic obstinacy, refused to accept the terms offered, hoping to exploit the divisions between parliament and the army. In June 1647, Cornet George Joyce seized the king from parliamentary custody on the army’s orders, and Charles was taken to the army’s headquarters at Newmarket.

Negotiations dragged on through 1647 and 1648. Charles negotiated simultaneously with the Scots, the English Presbyterians, and even the Irish Catholics — agreeing to preserve the Presbyterian church in Scotland for three years — in exchange for a Scottish invasion of England. This triggered the Second Civil War (1648), a series of desperate royalist risings accompanied by a Scottish invasion under the Duke of Hamilton. Cromwell crushed the Scots at Preston (August 1648) and the other risings were suppressed.

Trial and Execution (January 1649)

The army, now radicalised and led by Cromwell and his son-in-law Henry Ireton, determined that the king must be eliminated. On 6 December 1648, Colonel Thomas Pride purged the Long Parliament of members who favoured a negotiated settlement — Pride’s Purge. The remaining Rump of the Commons, about sixty members, voted to try the king for treason.

The High Court of Justice was established — 135 commissioners (though fewer than seventy ever sat). Charles was brought to Westminster Hall on 20 January 1649. When the charge was read, he refused to recognise the authority of the court. “I would know by what power I am called hither,” he said. “I would know by what authority — I mean lawful authority — the House of Commons — for I have heard of a king, and not of a House of Commons — but where are the Lords?”

Three times he was brought before the court; three times he declined to plead. The court pronounced sentence on 27 January: death by beheading.

On the morning of 30 January 1649, Charles was led from St James’s Palace to the Banqueting House in Whitehall. The scaffold had been erected outside. He wore two shirts against the cold, lest the shivering be mistaken for fear. His last words, addressed to Bishop Juxon, affirmed his innocence and his forgiveness of his enemies. He declared that he died “a Christian according to the profession of the Church of England, as I found it left me by my father.” He laid his neck on the block and, at one blow, the executioner severed his head.

A great groan rose from the crowd. Within days, the Rump Parliament abolished the monarchy and the House of Lords, and England became a commonwealth.

Character and Legacy

Hume’s character sketch of Charles I is one of the finest in English historical literature. The king was, Hume writes, “a good rather than a great man,” whose virtues predominated “extremely above his vices, or more properly speaking, his imperfections.” His dignity was free from pride, his humanity from weakness, his bravery from rashness, his temperance from austerity, his frugality from avarice. He was a devoted husband, an indulgent father, a patron of the arts — Van Dyck’s portraits of him have defined the image of Caroline kingship for posterity.

But Charles lacked the suppleness and dexterity necessary to navigate the constitutional crisis of his age. He believed deeply in the divine right of kings and could not bring himself to accept the diminution of his authority that parliament demanded. His political inflexibility, his tendency to choose the wrong advisers, and his ingrained distrust of compromise drove him into collision with forces he could neither control nor defeat. As Hume observes, “Exposed without revenue, without arms, to the assault of furious, implacable, and bigoted factions, it was never permitted him, but with the most fatal consequences, to commit the smallest mistake; a condition too rigorous to be imposed on the greatest human capacity.”

The accusation of insincerity often levelled at Charles is questioned by Hume. In every treaty, Charles refused to make concessions he could not in conscience maintain. The violations of the Petition of Right are ascribed by Hume “more to the necessity of his situation, and to the lofty ideas of royal prerogative which, from former established precedents, he had imbibed, than to any failure in the integrity of his principles.”

The legacy of Charles I is double. In defeat and death, he became a martyr — his Eikon Basilike (“The Royal Image”), purportedly his own meditations, became the most published book of the seventeenth century, fixing in the popular imagination the image of the saintly monarch betrayed by fanatics. At the same time, his reign demonstrated that the English constitution could not function without trust between crown and parliament, and that a king who would not govern with his people could not govern at all. The Restoration of 1660 would restore the monarchy, but it would never restore the untrammelled prerogative that Charles had claimed as his birthright.
