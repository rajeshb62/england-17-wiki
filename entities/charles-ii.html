Charles II of England

Summary

Charles II (r. 1660–1685), the eldest son of Charles I, was restored to the throne in 1660 after eleven years of republican government. His reign — the Restoration — was a period of profound contradiction: cultural renaissance and constitutional conflict, commercial expansion and military humiliation, an insatiable royal appetite for pleasure alongside a secret ambition to restore Catholicism and absolutism. The Merry Monarch who walked his spaniels in St James’s Park and fathered a dozen illegitimate children was also, as Hume describes him, a prince who “never said a foolish thing nor ever did a wise one.” By his death in 1685, Charles had outmanoeuvred his parliamentary opponents, crushed the Exclusion movement, and restored the vigour of the crown — but at the cost of a secret dependence on French subsidies, a ruined reputation for honesty, and a legacy of bitter partisan division between Whig and Tory that would define English politics for generations.

Exile and the Road to Restoration (1649–1660)

Upon the execution of Charles I on 30 January 1649, the Scots proclaimed his son King Charles II at Edinburgh. The young prince, then not yet nineteen, spent the next eleven years in exile — a wandering education in poverty, dependence, and dissimulation. He made a desperate gamble in 1650 by landing in Scotland and accepting the Presbyterian Covenant, but the Scottish army was crushed by Cromwell at Worcester (3 September 1651). Charles famously hid in an oak tree at Boscobel House before escaping to France after six weeks of fugitive wandering.

His exile was spent in the courts of France, the Spanish Netherlands, and the Dutch Republic — dependent on the charity of foreign princes, surrounded by a threadbare court of loyalists, and learning the arts of intrigue and patience. The death of Oliver Cromwell in September 1658 and the collapse of Richard Cromwell’s protectorate opened the door. General George Monck, commander of the English army in Scotland, marched south, restored the Rump Parliament, and then — crucially — declared for a free parliament. Monck opened secret communications with Charles through Sir John Granville and the Devonshire gentleman Morrice. Charles, following Monck’s advice that he leave Spanish territory to avoid arrest, escaped to Breda.

From Breda, Charles issued the Declaration of Breda (April 1660), promising a general pardon (excepting those to be named by parliament), liberty of conscience, the satisfaction of army arrears, and the confirmation of property settlements. The Convention Parliament, elected in a surge of royalist sentiment, voted unanimously to restore the king. Charles landed at Dover on 25 May 1660, entered London on his thirtieth birthday (29 May), and was crowned at Westminster Abbey on 23 April 1661. Burnet records that “a spirit of extravagant joy spread over the Nation, that brought on with it the throwing off the very professions of virtue and piety.” The Restoration was greeted with bonfires, the roasting of rumps in effigy, and a national determination to forget two decades of civil war.

The Clarendon Ministry (1660–1667)

The first years of Charles’s reign were dominated by Edward Hyde, Earl of Clarendon — the king’s lord chancellor and, in many respects, the architect of the Restoration settlement. Clarendon, a veteran of the Long Parliament and Charles I’s counsellor, was a man of great learning and magisterial temper. Burnet describes him as “an absolute favourite, and the chief or the only Minister, but with too magisterial a way.” He was “indefatigable in business” and a good chancellor, “only a little too rough, but very impartial in the administration of justice.”

The Convention Parliament (1660) enacted the Indemnity and Oblivion Act, which pardoned all crimes committed during the civil wars and interregnum — excepting only the regicides. Ten of the fifty-nine men who had signed Charles I’s death warrant were executed; the rest either fled or were imprisoned. The lands confiscated by the Commonwealth were restored to their original owners, and the Church of England was re-established with the Book of Common Prayer, episcopal government, and the Act of Uniformity (1662). Some two thousand Presbyterian ministers were ejected from their livings for refusing to conform — the Great Ejection — and the Clarendon Code of penal laws (the Corporation Act, Conventicle Act, Five Mile Act) was enacted to suppress Nonconformist worship.

The Cavalier Parliament, elected in 1661 and destined to sit for eighteen years, was overwhelmingly royalist and Anglican. It granted Charles a generous but inadequate revenue — about 1.2 million pounds per annum from customs and excise — far short of what was needed to govern without parliamentary supply. Charles married Catherine of Braganza, a Portuguese princess, in 1662, receiving as dowry the city of Tangier, the island of Bombay, and 300,000 pounds. The queen proved barren, producing no surviving heir, which cast a long shadow over the succession.

The Second Dutch War and National Disasters (1665–1667)

The commercial rivalry between England and the Dutch Republic, exacerbated by colonial conflicts in Guinea and North America, erupted into open war in 1665. The English fleet, under the command of the Duke of York (the king’s Catholic brother), defeated the Dutch at the Battle of Lowestoft (3 June 1665) — a victory that might have decided the war had the English pressed their advantage. But the year 1665 also brought the Great Plague of London, which killed an estimated 70,000 people. The court fled to Oxford, and commerce ground to a halt.

The war continued through 1666 with the epic Four Days’ Battle (1–4 June 1666), the longest naval engagement in the age of sail, in which the English under Prince Rupert and the Duke of Albemarle fought the Dutch under De Ruyter to a costly draw. The English lost some twenty ships and 5,000 men; the Dutch losses were comparable. In August, the English won a victory at St James’s Day Battle, but the war had exhausted both sides.

On 2 September 1666, the Great Fire of London broke out in a baker’s shop in Pudding Lane and raged for four days, destroying 13,200 houses, 87 parish churches, St Paul’s Cathedral, and most of the City within the walls. Charles and his brother James personally directed firefighting efforts. The fire was widely — and falsely — blamed on Catholic conspirators.

The financial strain of war, plague, and fire was unbearable. In June 1667, the Dutch delivered the final humiliation. De Ruyter’s fleet sailed up the River Medway, broke through the defensive chain at Chatham, and burned three capital ships of the English navy — including the Royal Charles, the very ship that had brought Charles back to England in 1660. Campbell’s naval history calls this “the most dishonourable to the English nation that perhaps ever happened in any reign.” The shock was profound. The Peace of Breda (July 1667) ended the war on largely status quo terms, but the damage to English prestige — and to Clarendon’s position — was irreparable.

The Fall of Clarendon and the Cabal Ministry (1667–1672)

Clarendon had long been unpopular — with the court for his moralising, with the Commons for his perceived arrogance, with the people for his defence of royal prerogative. The disasters of the Dutch War and the Medway humiliation proved fatal. Parliament impeached him; Charles, whose affection for his old chancellor had cooled, refused to save him. Clarendon fled to France in November 1667, where he lived in exile until his death in 1674, writing his great History of the Rebellion. Burnet observes that Charles, who had submitted entirely to Clarendon’s advice in the early years, had come to resent his minister’s “positive way.”

The ministry that succeeded Clarendon was known, from the initials of its five leading members, as the Cabal: Clifford, Arlington, Buckingham, Ashley (later Earl of Shaftesbury), and Lauderdale. It was a ministry of factions, not a coherent government. Hume writes that “if we consider the projects of the famous cabal, it will appear hard to determine, whether the end which those ministers pursued were more blamable and pernicious, or the means by which they were to effect it more impolitic and imprudent.”

The Cabal’s most notorious achievement was the Secret Treaty of Dover (1670), by which Charles agreed to join Louis XIV in war against the Dutch and, crucially, to declare himself a Catholic — in exchange for French subsidies of 200,000 pounds per year and the promise of 6,000 French troops to suppress any English rebellion. The treaty was negotiated in deepest secrecy by Charles, his sister Henrietta (the Duchess of Orleans), and four Catholic counsellors: Arlington, Arundel, Clifford, and Sir Richard Bellings. A sham treaty, omitting the religious clauses, was shown to the Protestant members of the Cabal — Buckingham, Ashley, and Lauderdale — to secure their participation in the war.

The Third Dutch War and the Test Act (1672–1674)

Charles launched the Third Dutch War in March 1672, in concert with France. The English fleet under the Duke of York fought the Dutch at Solebay (28 May 1672), a bloody and indecisive battle in which Lord Sandwich, the English admiral, was killed and his ship blown up. The war was deeply unpopular in England: the Dutch were fellow Protestants, the French were Catholic and absolutist, and the conflict was widely seen as a conspiracy to destroy English liberty.

In the same month, Charles issued the Declaration of Indulgence, suspending the penal laws against both Catholics and Nonconformists. Parliament, outraged, forced Charles to withdraw the Declaration and to assent to the Test Act (1673), which required all civil and military office-holders to receive the Anglican sacrament and to swear that they did not believe in transubstantiation. The Duke of York — a confessed Catholic — resigned his command of the Admiralty. The Cabal disintegrated: Clifford, a Catholic, resigned rather than take the test; Shaftesbury was dismissed and went into opposition; Buckingham fell from favour.

Charles made a separate peace with the Dutch at the Treaty of Westminster (1674), abandoned his French alliance for a time, and brought Sir William Temple and the Earl of Danby into government. Danby’s policy was to manage parliament through bribery and patronage, and to pursue a more Protestant foreign policy — including the marriage of Charles’s niece Mary to the Dutch Stadtholder, William of Orange (1677). But the king’s secret appetite for French money persisted, and he continued a covert correspondence with Louis XIV.

The Popish Plot and the Exclusion Crisis (1678–1681)

In 1678, the kingdom was convulsed by the Popish Plot — a fabrication by Titus Oates, a former Anglican clergyman turned Jesuit novice, who alleged a vast Catholic conspiracy to assassinate the king, burn London, and impose popery by French arms. Oates was, in Hume’s portrait, a man of “profligate character, mean birth, and infamous profession” — but his lurid accusations found ready believers in a nation already steeped in anti-Catholic paranoia.

The murder of Sir Edmund Bury Godfrey, the magistrate before whom Oates had sworn his deposition, was discovered in October 1678. Godfrey’s body was found in a ditch at Primrose Hill, run through with his own sword. Hume observes that “the dead body of Godfrey was regarded as the seal of the plot.” A wave of hysterical terror swept the nation. Fifteen innocent Catholics were executed on Oates’s perjured evidence. The Duke of York was forced into temporary exile in Brussels.

Shaftesbury, now leading the opposition in parliament, exploited the panic to launch the Exclusion Crisis — a concerted campaign to exclude the Catholic Duke of York from the succession. Three Exclusion Parliaments met between 1679 and 1681. The first passed an Exclusion Bill through the Commons, but it was defeated in the Lords. Charles dissolved parliament and called another; the bill passed the Commons again, only to be rejected once more. During this period, Charles created a new Privy Council under Sir William Temple’s scheme, including Shaftesbury as its president — but the arrangement lasted only months before Shaftesbury returned to open opposition.

The Habeas Corpus Act (1679) was passed in the midst of the crisis, strengthening the legal protection against arbitrary imprisonment. Charles gave reluctant assent, and it became one of the great constitutional foundations of English liberty. Meanwhile, the king’s natural son, the Duke of Monmouth — a handsome, popular Protestant — was put forward by the Exclusionists as a rival claimant. Monmouth made a triumphal progress through the west of England in 1680, collecting addresses from adoring crowds.

Charles outmanoeuvred his opponents by summoning a fourth parliament at Oxford in March 1681 — in the royalist heartland, away from the mob of London. When the Commons again pressed for exclusion, Charles dissolved parliament after only one week. He never called another.

The Tory Reaction and the Rye House Plot (1681–1685)

The dissolution of the Oxford Parliament marked the triumph of the royalist cause. A great wave of loyalist addresses poured in from the counties; the newly named Tories (as the court party was now called) routed the Whigs in the municipal elections of 1682; and the crown used quo warranto proceedings to strip London and other boroughs of their charters, remodelling their corporations in a loyalist interest. Charles, as Hume puts it, “was enabled to govern altogether without one” parliament.

The defeated Whigs, despairing of legal means, turned to conspiracy. The Rye House Plot (1683) was a scheme to assassinate Charles and his brother at the gate of a farmhouse called the Rye House in Hertfordshire, as they returned from the Newmarket races. The plot was discovered, and the government struck back ruthlessly. Lord William Russell, a popular Whig leader, was tried for high treason and executed in Lincoln’s Inn Fields (21 July 1683). Algernon Sidney, the republican theorist, was executed on 7 December 1683, convicted on the basis of his unpublished Discourses Concerning Government — an infamous abuse of the law of constructive treason. The Earl of Essex, arrested for complicity, cut his own throat in the Tower.

Hume judges that “the severities exercised during this part of the present reign were much contrary to the usual tenor of the king’s conduct.” Charles, in his last years, governed with a confidence he had never shown before. Monmouth, after a humiliating submission and recantation, was banished. The king received secret subsidies from Louis XIV, refused to call a parliament, and ruled through a Tory ministry dominated by the Earls of Halifax, Rochester, and Sunderland.

Death and Character

In February 1685, Charles was struck by a sudden apoplectic fit. He lingered for a few days, receiving Anglican clergymen with indifference — and then Catholic priests in secret, to whom he made his confession and received the last rites of the Roman Church. Two papers found in his cabinet, written in his own hand, contained arguments for the Catholic faith. He died on 6 February 1685, aged fifty-four, in the twenty-fifth year of his reign, and was buried in Westminster Abbey.

Hume’s character of Charles II is one of the great portraits of historical literature. As a companion, Charles was “the most amiable and engaging of men” — his wit “a plain, gaining, well-bred, recommending kind of wit,” his affability such that men “always went away contented both with him and with themselves.” As a sovereign, however, his record was darker: “Negligent of the interests of the nation, careless of its glory, averse to its religion, jealous of its liberty, lavish of its treasure, sparing only of its blood.” Hume attributes these failings chiefly to “the indolence of his temper — a fault which, however unfortunate in a monarch, it is impossible for us to regard with great severity.”

Burnet’s portrait is sharper. Charles had “a very good understanding” and “knew well the state of affairs both at home and abroad,” but was “liberal to excess” with fair promises “because he intended nothing by them.” He “seemed to have no sense of religion.” He hated business, delivering himself up “to a mad range of pleasure.” His first and longest mistress, Barbara Villiers (later Duchess of Cleveland), was “a woman of great beauty, but most enormously vitious and ravenous.” The ruin of his reign was “occasioned chiefly by his delivering himself up at his first coming over to a mad range of pleasure.”

Burnet records that Charles once told him he was no atheist, but “could not think God would make a man miserable only for taking a little pleasure out of the way.” He admired the French system of government and “thought a King who might be checkt, or have his Ministers called to an account by a Parliament, was but a King in name.” Yet he was a man of genuine intellectual curiosity: he understood mechanics, physics, and chemistry; was a good chemist “much set on several preparations of Mercury”; understood navigation and “the architecture of ships so perfectly, that in that respect he was exact rather more than became a Prince.”

Legacy

Charles II left an ambiguous inheritance. He restored the monarchy, the Church of England, and the social order that the civil wars had shattered. He presided over a cultural renaissance — the foundation of the Royal Society (1662), the flowering of Restoration drama (Dryden, Congreve, Wycherley), the architecture of Sir Christopher Wren, and the birth of the English navy as a permanent professional force. The Navigation Acts and the growth of colonial trade laid the foundations for England’s eighteenth-century commercial empire.

But he also left a poisoned constitution. His secret subsidy from France, his attempts to introduce Catholic toleration by prerogative, and his evasion of parliamentary government deepened the distrust between crown and Commons. The partisan categories forged in his reign — Whig and Tory, exclusionist and loyalist — would dominate English politics for two centuries. His brother James II inherited a throne vastly strengthened in power and prestige, but the religious and constitutional questions that Charles had suppressed rather than resolved would explode in the Glorious Revolution of 1688 within three years of his death.
