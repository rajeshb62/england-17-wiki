William III of England (William of Orange)

Summary

William III (r. 1689–1702), Prince of Orange and Stadtholder of the Dutch Republic before his English kingship, was the dominant figure of the late seventeenth century in Europe. Born to a nation fighting for its existence against Louis XIV, he became the lifelong champion of Protestant Europe against French hegemony. His invasion of England in 1688 — the last successful invasion of the island — overthrew James II and established a constitutional monarchy on foundations that endure to this day. Burnet, who sailed with him and knew him intimately, called him a prince of “great qualities” whose cold exterior concealed a profound dedication to the liberties of Europe. Macaulay, two centuries later, would rank him among the greatest of English sovereigns.

Youth and the Stadtholderate (1650–1672)

William Henry of Orange was born on 4 November 1650, a posthumous child: his father, William II of Orange, had died of smallpox eight days before the birth. His mother was Mary Stuart, eldest daughter of Charles I of England. The infant prince was born into a republic that had abolished the Stadtholderate after his father’s death, and he was raised under the guardianship of his mother and his grandmother, Amalia of Solms, in a household riven by political faction.

The education of the young prince was entrusted to Johan de Witt, the Grand Pensionary who had guided the Dutch Republic through the First and Second Anglo-Dutch Wars. De Witt, a republican who mistrusted the House of Orange as a threat to the States’ sovereignty, ensured that William’s upbringing was careful but restricted. The prince was taught mathematics, history, and the art of war, and showed early signs of that tenacity and reserve that would mark his mature character. He was a pale, asthmatic child, slight of frame but indomitable of spirit.

The year 1672 — the “Year of Disaster” — transformed his fortunes. Louis XIV, with Charles II of England as his ally, launched a devastating invasion of the Dutch Republic. The French army crossed the Rhine and swept through the provinces; three of the seven provinces were overrun. The Dutch people, in a panic, rose against the De Witt administration. Johan de Witt and his brother Cornelius were torn to pieces by an Hague mob. In the midst of this national crisis, the States General appointed the twenty-two-year-old Prince of Orange Stadtholder and Captain-General of the Union. Hume records that William was then in the twenty-second year of his age and already gave “strong indications of those great qualities by which his life was afterwards distinguished.”

The prince saved the republic by opening the dikes and flooding the Water Line — the great Dutch defence — and by conducting a dogged defensive campaign that convinced both France and England that the republic could not be conquered. By 1674 England had made a separate peace; by 1678 the war ended with the Treaty of Nimeguen, and William’s reputation as the foremost Protestant prince in Europe was established.

Marriage to Mary (1677)

William’s marriage to his cousin Mary, elder daughter of the Duke of York (the future James II), was a matter of high policy as much as personal attachment. Charles II, needing to reassure a parliament that distrusted his French and Catholic leanings, consented to the match, and the marriage was celebrated at St. James’s Palace on 4 November 1677 — William’s twenty-seventh birthday. Burnet, who was present, describes the princess as a woman of exemplary piety, beauty, and sweetness of temper. She was devoted to her husband from the first, and though the marriage was initially a political arrangement, it ripened into a genuine partnership that would carry them through the greatest crisis of the age.

The marriage had a double significance: it bound the House of Orange to the Stuart dynasty, and it placed William next in the English succession after James’s children. The birth of a son to James and Mary of Modena in June 1688 — the Prince of Wales — threatened to displace Mary and William from that succession and precipitated the crisis that followed.

The Invitation and the Invasion (1687–1688)

By 1687, James II’s Catholicising project had alienated every class of his subjects. The trial and acquittal of the Seven Bishops in June 1688 demonstrated the breadth of the opposition. The birth of the Prince of Wales transformed discontent into action. On 30 June 1688 — the very day the bishops were acquitted — seven men, known to history as the Immortal Seven, dispatched a formal invitation to William of Orange to intervene. The signatories included the Earl of Devonshire, the Earl of Danby, the Earl of Shrewsbury, Lord Lumley, the Bishop of London (Compton), Edward Russell, and Henry Sidney. They assured William that the army and nation were ready to rise.

William had been in communication with English malcontents for two years, but the invitation and the birth of the prince made delay impossible. He obtained the approval of the States General for an expedition of unprecedented scale: a fleet of nearly five hundred vessels, carrying an army of some fourteen thousand men, together with a train of artillery, horses, and stores. The States, Burnet writes, “fitted out a Fleet” that was “as great a one as had ever been set out by the Republic.”

The enterprise was hazardous in the extreme. Twice the fleet put to sea and was driven back by storms. A third attempt succeeded when a strong easterly wind — the “Protestant wind” — carried the armada down the English Channel and at the same time kept the English fleet bottled up in the Thames and the Downs. The Dutch ships passed the Straits of Dover in a great procession that took two days. On 5 November 1688 — the anniversary of the Gunpowder Plot — the fleet entered Torbay in Devon. Burnet, who sailed in the prince’s own ship, records that the landing was effected without opposition and that the whole coast of Devonshire declared for the prince within hours.

The Revolution and the Settlement (1688–1689)

William’s advance was a triumphal progress. The gentry and nobility flocked to his standard. The first great defection was that of Lord Cornbury with three regiments of dragoons. Then came John Churchill — the future Duke of Marlborough — who abandoned the king to whom he owed everything, carrying with him the Duke of Grafton and Colonel Berkeley. Prince George of Denmark, James’s son-in-law, deserted at Andover. On 25 November, Princess Anne fled London for Nottingham in the company of Lady Churchill, an act that broke James’s spirit. “God help me,” he cried, “my own children have forsaken me!”

James made a series of panicked concessions — dismissing the Ecclesiastical Commission, restoring the charters of London and the corporations, reinstating the fellows of Magdalen College — but they were received as signs of terror, not repentance. On 11 December he attempted flight, throwing the Great Seal into the Thames as he went. He was caught by fishermen at Faversham and brought back to London, where the populace unexpectedly cheered him; but William, who had no intention of allowing James to remain, ordered him to leave Whitehall. On 23 December 1688, James embarked for France, where Louis XIV received him with the utmost generosity at St. Germains.

William summoned a Convention Parliament, which met on 22 January 1689. After heated debate — especially over the word “abdication” versus “desertion” and the question of whether the throne was vacant — the Convention declared that James, having endeavoured to subvert the constitution and having withdrawn from the kingdom, had abdicated the government, and that the throne was thereby vacant. On 13 February 1689, William and Mary were offered the crown jointly, in accordance with the Declaration of Rights. William insisted on being king in his own right, not merely a regent or consort. Mary, with characteristic deference, made it known that she would accept no authority but from and through her husband. They were crowned together on 11 April 1689. The Declaration of Rights was later enacted as the Bill of Rights, which declared the dispensing and suspending power illegal, prohibited standing armies in peacetime without parliamentary consent, and settled the succession.

The War of the Grand Alliance and the Boyne (1689–1691)

The Revolution immediately involved England in the Nine Years’ War (1688–1697) against France. William, as Stadtholder, had been the soul of the Grand Alliance from the moment Louis XIV’s aggression had threatened the Dutch Republic; as King of England he commanded the resources of a second great power. His first and most urgent military task was Ireland, where James II had landed in March 1689 with French support and had summoned a Catholic parliament at Dublin.

William himself took command in Ireland in June 1690. Burnet records that he had 36,000 men in excellent condition, “full of heart and zeal.” He advanced from Belfast to the River Boyne near Drogheda in six days. King James had posted his Irish and French army — some 26,000 men — on the south side of the river. On 1 July 1690 (Old Style), William forced the passage. He was wounded by a cannonball on the eve of battle, but the injury was slight and he used it to rally his troops. The battle was a decisive victory: the Irish army was routed, James fled the field precipitately and returned to France, and Dublin fell without further resistance. The Boyne secured the Protestant ascendancy in Ireland for a century and confirmed William’s reputation as a soldier-king.

On the day before the Boyne, the English and Dutch fleets under the Earl of Torrington suffered a severe defeat at the Battle of Beachy Head. The French admiral Tourville exploited Torrington’s hesitation and the Dutch squadron’s bravery to inflict heavy losses. Torrington was court-martialled but acquitted — though he was never employed again. The defeat left the Channel temporarily open to French incursion, but the danger passed when Tourville failed to press his advantage.

La Hogue and the Naval War (1692–1694)

In 1692 the French prepared a full-scale invasion of England. A Jacobite army was assembled at La Hogue under the command of James II in person, with transports for 30,000 men. Admiral Tourville put to sea with a powerful fleet of 44 ships of the line. But the English and Dutch had now joined their forces under Admiral Edward Russell, who commanded a fleet of 99 sail.

The two fleets met off Cape Barfleur on 19 May 1692. After a fierce engagement, the French were forced to retreat through the Race of Alderney. While at anchor, their ships were attacked by English fireships, and twelve of the finest vessels in the French navy were burned or taken — six at La Hogue, five at Cherbourg, and others in the bay. Burnet records the rejoicing: “This victory seemed to fix the King more firmly on his throne, to the terror of all his enemies.” Campbell’s naval history gives the credit to Russell, whose “prudence, courage, and good conduct” had saved the kingdom. The Battle of La Hogue (or Barfleur) removed any serious French naval threat for the remainder of the war.

The Financial Revolution and the Bank of England (1694)

The cost of the war — with armies in Flanders, Ireland, and at sea — pressed heavily on the English Treasury. The old methods of parliamentary supply were inadequate. In 1694, William gave his assent to the bill creating the Governor and Company of the Bank of England — a private corporation that advanced 1.2 million pounds to the government at 8 percent interest, funded by a perpetual annual charge on certain taxes. The Bank became the engine of the financial revolution: it provided a permanent source of credit, stabilised the currency, and established the national debt on a solid foundation. Burnet notes that the Bank “quickly recovered its Credit so entirely, that there was no discount upon the Notes.” Though the Land Bank scheme that followed failed, the Bank of England proved one of the most durable institutions created in William’s reign, and it transformed England’s capacity to wage war.

Death of Queen Mary (1694)

On 28 December 1694, Queen Mary died of smallpox at Kensington Palace. She was thirty-two years old. Burnet, who attended her in her final illness, describes her death as a scene of extraordinary piety and composure. She had been the most popular figure of the reign — gracious, charitable, and devout — and her loss was felt throughout the kingdom. William was inconsolable. His grief was so profound that those who knew him wondered whether he would survive her. He shut himself up for weeks, refusing to see anyone. The asthmatic and consumptive state of his own health, which had long given uneasiness, became a source of general anxiety. Burnet observes that the King’s cold reserve and long hours in his closet were considerably worsened by the Queen’s death, which “seemed to break his spirit” and left him more solitary and withdrawn than ever. He would never marry again.

The Peace of Ryswick (1697)

The war dragged on for three more years after Mary’s death, with campaigns in Flanders that were costly and indecisive. William, despite his ill health, commanded the allied armies in person in each campaign. By 1696 both sides were exhausted. Negotiations opened at Ryswick, a palace near The Hague, and on 20 September 1697 a peace was signed. Louis XIV recognised William III as rightful King of England, agreed to provide no further assistance to James II, and restored most of the conquests he had made since the Treaty of Nimeguen. The peace was received in England with immense relief and rejoicing. For the first time in nine years, William could command the reduction of the army to a peace establishment — a step the Commons demanded and he reluctantly accepted.

The Act of Settlement (1701)

The great constitutional work of William’s reign was concluded in his last years. The death in 1700 of Princess Anne’s last surviving child, the Duke of Gloucester, threw the succession into peril. William pressed Parliament to settle the Protestant succession beyond all dispute. The result was the Act of Settlement of 1701, which provided that the crown should pass, after Anne, to the Electress Sophia of Hanover — a granddaughter of James I — and her Protestant descendants. The Act excluded Catholics and those who married Catholics from the throne, required future monarchs to join in communion with the Church of England, and placed important restrictions on the royal prerogative. Burnet records that William himself instructed him to propose the naming of the “Dutchess of Hanover and her Posterity” in the House of Lords, and that the King “signified his pleasure in this also to the Ministers.” The Act of Settlement remains, alongside Magna Carta and the Bill of Rights, one of the fundamental statutes of the British constitution.

Death and Character

William III died at Kensington Palace on 8 March 1702. His health had been declining for years: he suffered from asthma, dropsy, and a persistent cough. On 20 February 1702, his horse Sorrel stumbled on a molehill in Hampton Court Park and threw the King, fracturing his collarbone. The injury was not thought serious at first, but his constitution was too broken to recover. He rallied briefly, then sank into a fever and died in the arms of his physician. He was buried in Westminster Abbey.

The character of William III, as drawn by those who knew him, is a study in contrasts. Burnet, who had every opportunity of observing him at close quarters, paints him as a prince of profound abilities but a “cold and reserved temper” that kept even his closest servants at a distance. He was apt to be “peevish,” and his long hours in his closet, combined with his constitutional reserve, gave him an air of melancholy that was often mistaken for pride. He valued secrecy and was slow to confide his plans to anyone, but once he had taken a resolution, he adhered to it with immovable firmness. In the field he was brave to the point of recklessness, sharing the hardships of his soldiers and exposing himself freely to enemy fire. He had little of the graces of a court — he spoke slowly and with difficulty, disliked ceremony, and was indifferent to the arts — but he had an unerring sense of the great forces at work in European politics and the patience to wait for his opportunities. His whole life was a single-minded struggle against the ambition of Louis XIV. “He seemed to have nothing else in his view,” Burnet writes, “but to check the growing greatness of France and to secure the liberties of Europe.”

Hume’s judgment, though briefer, is no less measured. He notes that William’s reign established the revolution settlement on a foundation of law, that his wars laid the basis for England’s emergence as a European great power, and that his financial innovations gave the monarchy the resources it needed without the arbitrary methods of his predecessors. The cost was a war debt that burdened the nation for generations, but the achievement was the preservation of a free constitution and a Protestant succession against the most formidable monarchy in Europe.

Legacy

William III transformed the English state. The Bill of Rights, the Toleration Act, the Bank of England, the national debt, the Act of Settlement, and the standing army all date from his reign. He gave England the constitutional framework within which parliamentary government would develop in the eighteenth century, and he placed the kingdom at the head of the European coalition that finally checked the power of Louis XIV. The Glorious Revolution that he led — bloodless in England, costly in Ireland — became the central myth of Whig constitutionalism: the justification for limited monarchy, the rule of law, and the Protestant succession. For the Jacobites he was “the usurper,” the little Dutchman who stole a crown; for posterity he was the king who saved English liberty from the fate that awaited it under James II. His name is remembered in the annual celebration of the Orange Order, in the institutions he founded, and in the constitution he established. He was the last successful invader of England, and by that invasion he shaped the history of Britain and Europe for centuries to come.
