East India Company

The Governor and Company of Merchants of London Trading into the East Indies, chartered by Queen Elizabeth on 31 December 1600, was by the late 17th century the most powerful chartered trading company in England. The company’s late-Stuart history was marked by scandal, political polarisation, a rival company, and a final merger that produced a single joint-stock corporation uniting the two interests.

Restoration and Charles II

Under Charles II the company reached the height of its power. Hume records that Charles II revived and supported the company’s charter, while Campbell notes that the East India Company were exceedingly favoured and protected under Charles II, especially at the start of his reign. The king’s support was critical: the company’s charter gave it monopoly rights over English trade east of the Cape of Good Hope, and royal favour protected it against the attacks of interlopers and free-trade advocates.

By the 1680s the company’s stock was already large, and its Governor, Sir Josiah Child, was one of the wealthiest merchants in London. Child himself records that the company could take up whatever money it pleased at four per cent interest — a mark of the enormous credit it commanded in the City. Yet even at this height, the foundations of the company’s political position were fragile: its monopoly depended on the Crown’s goodwill, and every change in the political wind threatened its privileges.

The Monied Company of the Whig Interest

By the 1690s the East India Company had become a deeply political institution. Its stock was held by City of London merchants and financiers aligned with the Whig interest, alongside the Bank of England, and the two were often spoken of together as the pillars of the new financial order. Burnet records that opponents of the King’s ministry argued that the Bank of England and the new East-India Company, being in Whig hands, would command all the money and trade of England. The company’s connection to the court and the Whig ministry made it both powerful and vulnerable: every attack on the ministry was also an attack on the company, and every company scandal was ammunition for the Tories.

The company was governed by a Governor, Deputy Governor, and Court of Directors, elected by the freemen of the company. Sir Josiah Child, the leading economic writer of the age, served as Governor and was the company’s most able public defender. In his Discourse of Trade (1698), Child argued that a joint-stock company was essential for long-distance, high-risk trades that required years to return a profit, and that without the company the trade itself would not exist. He noted that any Englishman could buy into the company by purchasing an action for a modest five-pound admission fee — a far more open structure than the company’s earlier practice of charging a fifty-pound fine for admission.

The Bribery Scandal of 1695

In 1695, a parliamentary inquiry into the East India Company revealed a systematic programme of secret payments to Members of Parliament. Burnet writes that the company’s books recorded 170,000 pounds paid for secret services, most of which was believed to have gone to members of the House of Commons. For the two preceding winters there had been attempts to break the company and open a free trade to the Indies, and the company appears to have used bribes to defeat these efforts. Burnet observes that some of the fiercest opponents of the company insensibly fell off from that opposition and turned to serve it — notably Sir Edward Seymour, who was said to have received 12,000 pounds under the pretext of a bargain for their saltpetre.

The scandal widened when it emerged that the company had also attempted to bribe the Lord President (the Duke of Leeds) to procure a new charter. One Firebras, a merchant employed by the company, had treated with a certain Bates, a friend of the Marquess of Caermarthen, and Bates was to have five thousand guineas for procuring a new charter. Bates later swore he had offered the money to the Lord President, who refused it but advised him to keep it. The House of Commons voted an impeachment for misdemeanour against the Lord President, who saved himself by a speech before the House.

The scandal gave the company’s enemies powerful ammunition. The King, upon an address from the Commons, granted the old company a new charter — but with a condition that they take in a new subscription of 700,000 pounds to increase their stock and trade, and with a clause reserving to the Crown the power to dissolve them upon three years’ notice.

The New East India Company (1698)

The great crisis came in 1698. The government, desperate for funds to pay the arrears of the army and the navy, received a proposition for constituting a new East-India Company that would trade with a joint stock while admitting others in a determinate proportion to a separate trade. The projectors of the new company offered two million pounds at eight per cent interest, on the security of a good fund. The old company opposed this and offered a sum of their own — but far short of what the public occasions required.

Burnet describes how the old company belatedly offered the whole sum, but the party was already formed and they came too late. The proceeding was denounced as rigorous, if not unjust, and threw the old company into the hands of the Tories, making a great breach in the City of London.

The Act for the new company was carried, though the King himself was very indifferent in the matter at first. A new corporation was erected — the English Company Trading to the East Indies — which immediately began to trade in competition with the old company. The result was a decade of ruinous rivalry: two English companies simultaneously operating factories in India, undercutting each other’s prices, and confusing both the Mughal authorities and English merchants.

Electoral Corruption and Parliamentary Inquiries (1701)

The rivalry between the two companies poisoned English politics. In the general election of 1701, reports were brought of elections scandalously purchased by the new East-India Company. Instead of the drinking and entertainments by which elections were formerly managed, a new practice of buying votes appeared, with electors subscribing to choose a blank person before knowing the candidate’s name.

Burnet contrasts the methods: the old company had driven a course of corruption within doors with little shame, while the new company bought elections themselves rather than buying the persons already elected. Several elections were declared void, and some of the persons so chosen were imprisoned and expelled the House — though Burnet notes great partiality appeared in which cases were prosecuted.

When the new parliament met, some of its members tried to shake the Act made in favour of the new East-India Company. Burnet denounces this as contrary to the fundamental maxims of the constitution: money raised by parliament on bargains that were performed gave those who advanced it a sacred purchase on those acts; to overturn it would destroy all credit.

Trade and Economics

Gregory King’s trade statistics for the 1680s give the fullest picture of the East India trade’s scale and structure.

Original exports (money or wares)500,000 pounds
Re-exports of European/African/plantation goods20,000
Total exports520,000
Imports in gross1,700,000
General increase (excess of imports over exports)1,260,000

National profit after freight480,000

The trade was generating between double and treble its exports in gross profit. But the East India trade was extraordinarily expensive to conduct: freight consumed 62.5 per cent of the general increase, compared to only 12-13 per cent for European, African and Levant trades, and 26-30 per cent for the plantation trade. After freight, the national profit on the East India trade was roughly equal to the value of exports — whereas the plantation trade returned almost double, and the European trades four to five times their exports.

For comparison across trade branches:

  • Exports to East Indies + plantations: barely one-fourth of European/African/Levant exports
  • Imports from East Indies + plantations: two-thirds as large as European/African/Levant imports

The ratio of imports to exports shifted drastically with distance. In the infancy of the East India trade (1613), England had re-exported 200,000 pounds in pepper alone. By King’s time (circa 1688), re-exports from India in pepper, saltpetre, calicoes, silks, drugs, diamonds, and pearls reached 800,000 pounds annually.

Child, in his defence of the company, acknowledged the charge that the East-India Company imported much more than it exported, and carried out quantities of gold and silver annually to pay for its purchases — the classic mercantilist objection to the East India trade. His answer was that the Dutch esteemed the East India trade more profitable than the mines of gold and silver in America to the King of Spain. The real measure of a trade was not the immediate balance of bullion but the ultimate increase in national wealth through re-export and the stimulation of shipping, insurance, and ancillary industries.

The company also provided critical services to the Crown beyond its commercial functions. In 1699, when pirates in the Indian seas — mostly English — robbed ships of the Mughal and risked reprisals against the company’s factories, the East-India Company having represented the danger of the Mogul’s taking reprisals the government authorised an expedition to destroy the pirates’ bases in Madagascar. Captain Kidd was commissioned for this service, though the expedition was funded by private subscription rather than public money.

The Company and the Darien Scheme

The East India Company’s monopoly was a direct cause of the Darien disaster. When the Company of Scotland (the Darien Company) was projected, its establishment was seen in England as a threat to the East India Company’s exclusive trade. Dalrymple records that the houses of lords and commons on 13 December 1695 concurred in a joint address against the establishment of the Darien company, as detrimental to the East India company’s interest. The Commons even impeached some of their own countrymen for being instrumental in erecting the Scottish company — arraigning the subjects of another country for making use of the laws of their own.

Campbell, writing later and more dispassionately, describes the episode from the Scottish perspective. The Revolution of 1688 had brought back to Scotland several worthy patriots who procured an act in 1693 for the encouragement of foreign commerce, and another in 1695 for setting up a Scottish East India Company. Subscriptions were taken not only in Scotland but also at London, Hamburg, and Amsterdam, in which they were sufficiently supported by both the royal and legislative authority However, the English and Dutch East India Companies took umbrage and used their influence with their respective governments to obstruct the subscriptions. This forced King William into an impossible position: he was king of Scotland, which had sanctioned the company, but also king of England and Stadtholder of Holland, both of which opposed it. The affair forced him to dismiss two able ministers — the Marquis of Tweedale and Secretary Johnston — for having given the royal assent to the act establishing the company.

This English hostility, driven in part by the East India Company’s influence, was a major cause of the Darien Scheme’s failure and of the deep Scottish resentment that paved the way for the Union of 1707.

The Rival Companies in India

The creation of the new East India Company in 1698 did not merely create competition in London — it extended to India itself. The new company sent Sir William Norris as ambassador to the Great Mogul to promote its interests and obtain trading privileges. But the old company, being already in possession of the forts and factories in the East Indies, prepossessed the Mogul and other princes against the new company, so that the ambassador was indifferently received and the affair miscarried. The old company’s established presence on the ground — its factories at Surat, Madras, Bombay, and Hugli — proved an insuperable advantage.

Dalrymple, in a panegyric to Charles Montague inserted in his narrative, records that Montague was the man who erected the Bank of England, gave wings to public credit, established the East India Company on a firm basis, and settled the House of Hanover on the throne. The connection between the three great Whig achievements — the Bank, the re-founded East India Company, and the Protestant succession — was explicit in the minds of contemporaries.

The Union of the Companies

Under Queen Anne, the two rival companies were eventually merged into a single corporation — the United Company of Merchants of England Trading to the East Indies. The index to Burnet’s second volume records that the companies joined — a reference to the Act of Union of the two East India Companies passed in 1702 and finally implemented in 1709, the same year the Bank’s fund was enlarged. The merged company took the form that would endure for the next century and a half, with a single joint-stock, a unified Court of Directors, and a charter that confirmed its exclusive right to trade east of the Cape of Good Hope.

The union resolved the ruinous competition and restored the company’s credit, but the political divisions of the 1698 split had lasting consequences. Burnet’s reflection on the episode captures the transformation: the new financial institutions — the Bank, the East India Company, and the national debt — had engaged all those concerned in them to be, upon the account of their own interest, zealous for maintaining the Government. The East India Company, once a mere commercial venture, had become one of the pillars of the post-Revolution fiscal-military state.
