William Laud, Archbishop of Canterbury

Summary

William Laud (1573–1645), Archbishop of Canterbury from 1633 until his impeachment by the Long Parliament, was the architect of the Arminian and ceremonial revival that provoked the Puritan rebellion and hastened the English Civil War. Hume describes him as a man of learning, severity, and unrelenting zeal — “virtuous, if severity of manners alone, and abstinence from pleasure, could deserve that name” — whose passion for ritual order and priestly authority made him the most hated man in England outside the court. Burnet calls him “a learned, a sincere and zealous man, regular in his own life, and humble in his private deportment; but was a hot, indiscreet man.” His prosecution and execution in January 1645, after four years in the Tower, were the second great act of parliamentary vengeance (after Strafford’s) and a decisive step toward the destruction of the established church.

Early Career at Oxford

Laud was born at Reading, Berkshire, in 1573, the son of a clothier. He was educated at Reading School and St John’s College, Oxford, where he took his BA in 1594 and was elected a fellow. He was ordained and rose rapidly in academic administration, becoming President of St John’s College in 1611. At Oxford he became known for his opposition to the dominant Calvinist orthodoxy and for his attachment to the ceremonies and liturgy of the primitive church. In 1616, James I appointed him Dean of Gloucester. There he immediately ordered the communion table, which Puritan reformers had moved into the chancel, to be replaced at the east end of the cathedral — an early sign of the policy he would later impose on the whole kingdom.

Bishop of St David’s and London

Laud was consecrated Bishop of St David’s in 1621, but his influence remained modest until the accession of Charles I. The new king shared Laud’s reverence for ecclesiastical order and his suspicion of Puritan theology. In 1627, Laud was appointed Bishop of Bath and Wells, and in 1628 he was translated to the wealthy see of London. From this position he became the king’s principal adviser on church affairs, eclipsing the more moderate Archbishop Abbot.

Arminian Theology and Opposition to Calvinism

The theological controversy that defined Laud’s career was the conflict between Calvinism and Arminianism. The English Reformation had been built on the Calvinist doctrines of predestination and absolute decrees — tenets embedded in the Thirty-Nine Articles and preached by the first reformers. The Dutch theologian Arminius and his followers had challenged this system, arguing for human free will and a conditional election. Hume records that the Arminians, “finding more encouragement from the superstitious spirit of the church than from the fanaticism of the Puritans, gradually incorporated themselves with the former.” Laud, along with Bishops Neile and Montague, became the leader of this party. The Commons, dominated by the Puritan faction, saw the spread of Arminianism as a mortal threat and repeatedly censured its adherents. Laud’s identification with Arminianism tied him, in the Puritan mind, to Rome itself.

Archbishop of Canterbury (1633)

Upon Abbot’s death in 1633, Laud was appointed Archbishop of Canterbury. He now controlled the ecclesiastical patronage of the entire realm and used it ruthlessly to promote high-church clergymen and to suppress Puritan lecturers. Hume observes that Laud “seems not to have been altogether so great an enemy to the Catholics” as his predecessors — an indulgence that, together with his severity toward Puritans, aroused the deepest suspicion. The court of Rome twice offered him a cardinal’s hat, which he declined, saying “something dwelt within him which would not suffer his compliance, till Rome were other than it is.” A court lady, asked by Laud why she had turned Catholic, replied: “I perceive your grace and many others are making haste to Rome; and therefore, in order to prevent my being crowded, I have gone before you.”

The Laudian Church: Beauty of Holiness and Altar Rails

Laud’s programme for the church was one of uniformity, decency, and what might be called the “beauty of holiness.” He ordered the communion table — which had stood in the middle of the chancel since the Reformation — to be removed to the east end of every church, railed in, and denominated an altar. The officiating clergyman was to be called a priest. Copes and other embroidered vestments were to be worn in administering the sacrament. Bowing toward the altar was required. Crucifixes and painted windows were encouraged. Burnet records that Laud’s innovations included “setting the communion table by the east walls of churches, bowing to it, and calling it the Altar,” the suppression of lectureships, and the encouragement of Sunday sports.

The consecration of St Catherine’s Church in 1630 became a cause celebre. As described by Hume, Laud approached the west door and a voice cried: “Open, open, ye everlasting doors, that the king of glory may enter in.” He fell on his knees, pronounced the ground holy, several times took up dust and threw it in the air, bowed repeatedly toward the communion table, marched around the church reciting psalms, and pronounced imprecations upon those who would profane the place. To the Puritans, these ceremonies were indistinguishable from popish superstition.

Persecution of Puritans: Prynne, Bastwick, and Burton

Laud’s enforcement of uniformity was most notoriously displayed in the Star Chamber prosecutions of the Puritan writers Prynne, Bastwick, and Burton. Prynne, a barrister of Lincoln’s Inn, had published a thousand-page quarto called “Histrio-Mastyx,” attacking stage-plays, music, dancing, and Christmas-keeping, but also the hierarchy, ceremonies, and innovations of Laud. He was sentenced to the pillory in two places, the loss of both ears, a 5000 pound fine, and imprisonment for life.

In 1637, Burton (a divine) and Bastwick (a physician) were tried in the Star Chamber for seditious and schismatical libels against the church, and condemned to the same punishment. Prynne was tried again for a new offence and lost what remained of his ears. Hume notes that the Star Chamber’s severity was “generally ascribed to Laud’s passionate disposition.” The public was deeply offended, and the alacrity with which the three men suffered increased still further the indignation against the archbishop.

The Scottish Prayer Book Crisis

The crisis that ultimately destroyed Laud and his king began in Scotland. Determined to impose liturgical uniformity across both kingdoms, Laud and Charles introduced a new Prayer Book and canons for the Church of Scotland in 1637 — a liturgy modelled on the English Book of Common Prayer but even more ceremonial. When the dean of Edinburgh attempted to read the new service at St Giles’ Cathedral on 23 July 1637, a woman named Jenny Geddes is said to have thrown her stool at the pulpit, sparking a full-scale riot. The National Covenant was signed in February 1638, binding the Scots to resist the innovations. A general assembly abolished episcopacy. War followed; and the financial necessity of suppressing the Scottish rebellion forced Charles to summon the Short Parliament (1640) and then the Long Parliament — which would turn on Laud and Strafford with deadly determination.

Impeachment, Imprisonment, and Trial

The Long Parliament met in November 1640 in a fury against the policies of the Personal Rule. Pym and the popular leaders moved quickly. On 18 December 1640, after a deliberation of scarcely half an hour, the Commons voted an impeachment of high treason against the Archbishop. Laud, sequestered from Parliament and committed to the custody of the gentleman usher of the black rod, was sent to the Tower of London. Burnet records that when the news came, Laud’s secretary was busy for three hours destroying papers from his closet, including — it is believed — the original Magna Carta.

Laud remained in the Tower for nearly four years while the Commons, engaged in the greater enterprises of war, found no leisure to finish his impeachment. Finally, after the alliance with the Scottish Covenanters revived the spirit of Puritan vengeance, he was brought to trial. Hume writes that after examining over 150 witnesses, the Commons found so little likelihood of obtaining a judicial sentence that they “were obliged to have recourse to their legislative authority, and to pass an ordinance for taking away the life of this aged prelate.” The peers, threatened by the London mob, passed the bill of attainder by a vote of only seven — the rest absenting themselves from shame or fear.

Execution (10 January 1645)

Laud was brought to the scaffold on Tower Hill on 10 January 1645. He had behaved during his trial with spirit and vigour, and he met his death with composure. Though he had often professed a fear of violent death, Hume records that “he found all his fears to be dissipated before that superior courage by which he was animated.” On the scaffold, he was harassed by Sir John Clotworthy, a Puritan zealot, who attempted to trepan him into confessing that he trusted for salvation to good works rather than the death of the Redeemer. Having extricated himself from these theological toils, he laid his head on the block, and it was severed at one blow.

Burnet notes that the manner of his death, so unjust and violent, “raised his character, and established all his notions as standards, by which judgments are to be made of men whether they are true to the church or not.” Hume observes that the execution of this old and inoffensive prisoner “can be ascribed to nothing but vengeance and bigotry in those severe religionists, by whom the parliament was entirely governed.”

Character and Legacy

Laud’s character was the subject of fierce dispute in his own time and ever since. Hume’s portrait is unsparing: zealous, efficient, and utterly convinced of his own rectitude, Laud “overlooked every human consideration” in pursuit of his holy purposes, “the heat and indiscretion of his temper made him neglect the views of prudence and rules of good manners.” His enemies were, in his own eyes, the enemies of God and the king; every exercise of his anger became in his eyes a merit and a virtue.

Burnet offers a more measured assessment: Laud was “a learned, sincere and zealous man, regular in his own life,” but one who “eagerly pursued some matters that were either very inconsiderable or mischievous” — the position of the communion table, bowing to the altar, suppressing lectures, encouraging Sunday sports. “All the zeal and heat of that time was laid out on these.”

By imposing a high ceremonialism on a nation that had been raised on Calvinist austerity, by persecuting the Puritan gentry who dominated the House of Commons, and by pressing liturgical uniformity on the Scots, Laud united against the crown the very forces that would destroy it. He was, in Hume’s phrase, the man “who acquired so great an ascendancy over Charles, and who led him, by the facility of his temper, into a conduct which proved so fatal to himself and to his kingdoms.”
