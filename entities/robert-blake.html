Robert Blake

Summary

Robert Blake (1598–1657), General at Sea of the Commonwealth of England, was the first great commander of the English professional navy and the man who established the tradition of naval dominance that would be perfected by Nelson a century and a half later. A land officer past fifty when first sent to sea, he learned the art of naval war with astonishing speed, defeated the Dutch in the battles of the First Anglo-Dutch War, burned the Barbary corsairs in their own harbours, and destroyed the Spanish treasure fleet in the Bay of Santa Cruz — an action that Lord Clarendon called the boldest ever attempted. Hume called him “the most perfect character of the age, and the least stained with those errors and violences which were then so predominant.”

Early Life as a Merchant and Soldier

Robert Blake was born at Bridgwater, Somerset, in August 1598, the eldest son of a substantial merchant family. He was educated at Oxford, where he entered St Alban’s Hall in 1615, but left without a degree on the death of his father. Returning to Bridgwater, he engaged in trade as a merchant; little is known of this period of his life, though his fellow townsmen held him in sufficient regard to elect him to the Short Parliament of 1640 as burgess for Bridgwater. He was returned again for the same borough to the Long Parliament.

When civil war broke out in 1642, Blake, then forty-four years old and without any military experience, accepted a captain’s commission in the parliamentary army. His aptitude for war proved immediate. He was given the command of the garrisons of Lyme Regis and later Taunton, where he displayed the unyielding obstinacy that would mark his naval career. At Taunton, Hume records, he suffered a long siege from Sir Richard Granville, who commanded an army of some eight thousand men; and though the defence had been obstinate, the garrison was reduced to the last extremity. Fairfax’s relief of Taunton in 1645 preserved Blake’s command and his reputation. His resistance at Lyme had already frustrated Prince Maurice’s royalist forces in the West Country.

Appointed General at Sea

In February 1649, after the execution of Charles I, the Commonwealth parliament removed the Earl of Warwick from the command of the fleet and replaced him with three colonels — Blake, Deane, and Popham — who were given the title “Generals at Sea.” Campbell records that the fleet “was put into the hands of land-officers, such as Blake, Deane, and Popham, who, notwithstanding, behaved well, quickly gained the love of the sailors, and grew in a short time very knowing seamen themselves.”

Blake’s first commission was to pursue Prince Rupert, whose squadron of some twenty-five ships had deserted the parliament and was raiding English commerce from the port of Kinsale, in Ireland. Blake and Popham were ordered “to block up the prince’s squadron in the port of Kinsale, which they accordingly did, and reduced them to such extremities, that his men began to desert in great numbers.” Rupert forced his way out on 24 October 1649 with the loss of three ships and sailed for the Mediterranean.

The Pursuit of Prince Rupert

Blake pursued Rupert to the coast of Portugal. The prince took shelter in the Tagus; the king of Portugal, favouring the royal cause, refused Blake admittance. Blake responded by seizing twenty Portuguese ships richly laden — a decision that compelled the king of Portugal to negotiate with the Commonwealth. Blake wrote a letter in strong terms to the king of Spain demanding the delivery of any of Rupert’s ships that might fall into Spanish hands; the Spanish king returned a very civil answer and sent Blake a ring worth fifteen hundred pounds.

In the river of Lisbon, in September and October 1650, Blake ruined the Brazil fleet, which induced the Portuguese to force Rupert out of their port. The prince fled to Carthagena; Blake pursued him thither, but shortage of provisions compelled the English admiral to put to sea, and Rupert escaped to Malaga. A general terror, Campbell notes, was occasioned in the courts of Spain and Portugal by Blake’s destruction of Rupert’s fleet. Both powers dispatched ambassadors to England “to acknowledge the power of the parliament.” The reduction of the Scilly Isles and Jersey followed in 1651, securing the sovereignty of the narrow seas for the Commonwealth.

The First Anglo-Dutch War

The Battle of Dover, 19 May 1652

The First Anglo-Dutch War began with an encounter between Blake and the celebrated Dutch admiral Maarten Tromp. Tromp, with a fleet of forty-two sail, appeared in the Downs and, according to his own account, was forced in by stress of weather. Blake, with only fifteen ships, fired at his flag three times; Tromp returned a broadside. The battle lasted from four in the afternoon till nine at night; Bourne joined Blake with eight ships, and the Dutch bore away. Blake’s victory, against such odds, was celebrated throughout England. The States-General, “so sensible of their being in the wrong, and at the same time so mortified that their fleet, notwithstanding its superiority, had been beaten,” sent ambassadors to treat — but the terms demanded were too high, and war was proclaimed in Holland on 8 July 1652.

The Battle of Kentish Knock, 28 September 1652

Blake, now commanding about sixty ships, engaged the Dutch fleet under De Witte near the Kentish Knock. He divided his fleet into three squadrons, commanded by himself, Vice-admiral Penn, and Rear-admiral Bourne. After a sharp engagement during which the Dutch were roughly handled — one of their admirals was taken, two ships sunk, and a third blown up — De Witte retired. Blake pursued as far as twelve leagues from the Dutch shore, then returned in triumph to the Downs. The Dutch lost no fewer than twenty captains who “withdrew out of the line of battle.”

The Battle of Dungeness, 29 November 1652

Blake had detached twenty ships to convoy the Newcastle colliers, twelve more to Plymouth, and fifteen were in the river for repairs. Tromp, learning that Blake had only thirty-seven ships, attacked him off Dungeness with seventy sail. Blake fought with desperate courage. His own ship, the Triumph, the Victory, and the Vanguard bore the whole weight of the Dutch attack. The Garland and the Bonaventure were taken after their captains were killed. Blake pushed so far to their relief that he was nearly taken himself. He was forced to retreat to the Thames. Tromp sailed into the Channel with a broom at his topmast-head, signifying he would sweep the English from the seas. The parliament, showing steadiness, caressed Blake after his defeat and named him, in conjunction with Deane and Monk, General at Sea for another year.

The Battle of Portland, 18–20 February 1653

In six weeks the Commonwealth fitted out a fleet of sixty sail. Blake and Deane, on board the Triumph, led twelve stout ships and fell in with Tromp on 18 February. Blake was wounded in the thigh by a piece of iron; the same shot tore Deane’s coat and breeches. Captain Ball was killed at Blake’s feet; his secretary was shot receiving orders. A hundred seamen were lost on the Triumph alone. The battle raged for three days. The Dutch lost eleven men of war and thirty merchantmen. Blake had used soldiers on board his ships to good effect. Though Blake was badly wounded, the Dutch were clearly beaten.

The Battle of the Gabbard (North Foreland), 2–3 June 1653

Monk and Deane commanded the English fleet of ninety-five sail. On the first day, the brave Admiral Deane was cut in two by a chain-shot at the start of the action; Monk, with great presence of mind, covered the body and kept the flag flying, so the fleet did not know of the loss. The Dutch fell into confusion. That night Blake arrived with a squadron of eighteen ships and took part in the second day’s engagement. The fight resumed at eight in the morning with the utmost fury. Vice-admiral Penn boarded Tromp twice. A general flight followed. The Dutch lost six ships sunk, two blown up, and eleven taken — including a vice-admiral and two rear-admirals — while the English lost Admiral Deane and one captain. The Dutch ports were now blocked; the sight of a foreign fleet at their doors excited domestic tumults in Holland and compelled the States to sue for peace.

Tromp was killed in the final battle of the war off the Texel on 31 July 1653, in which Monk commanded. Peace was concluded on 4 April 1654, with the Dutch acknowledging the dominion of the English at sea, consenting to strike the flag, and submitting to the Navigation Act.

The Mediterranean Campaign: Barbary Pirates

After the Dutch war, Admiral Blake was sent into the Mediterranean in the summer of 1654 with a great fleet. His orders, to be opened at sea, were to “procure satisfaction from such princes and states as had either insulted the government, or injured the commerce of England.” He first visited Leghorn, where he compelled the Grand Duke of Tuscany to pay sixty thousand pounds compensation for damages suffered by English shipping during the Dutch war.

Thence he proceeded to Algiers, arriving 10 March 1655, and demanded satisfaction for piracy and the release of English captives. The Dey temporised. Blake sailed to Tunis, where he sent a similar message and received the defiant answer: “Here are our castles of Guletta and Porto Ferino: you may do your worst; we do not fear you.” Blake entered the bay of Porto Ferino, came within musket-shot of the castle and lines, and so warmly plied them with cannon that the fortifications were soon in a defenceless condition. He resolved to burn the nine Tunisian ships in the road. Every captain manned his long-boat with choice men; these entered the harbour and fired the corsair ships while the fleet covered them from the castle. All the ships were burnt with the loss of only twenty-five men killed and forty-eight wounded. “This daring action spread the terror of his name through Africa and Asia,” writes Campbell, “which had long been formidable in Europe.” From Tunis Blake sailed to Tripoli and concluded a peace with that government; returning to Tunis, he compelled the inhabitants to sue for peace on terms “mortifying to them, glorious for him, and profitable for his country.”

The Destruction of the Spanish Fleet at Santa Cruz de Tenerife, April 1657

The war with Spain having begun, Blake cruised before Cadiz through the winter of 1656–57. In April 1657 he learned that a Spanish treasure fleet — six great galleons richly laden and ten other vessels — had put into the Bay of Santa Cruz in the island of Tenerife. He immediately sailed thither.

The bay was secured by a strong castle well supplied with artillery, seven forts united by a line of communication lined with musketeers, and a barricado boom before the port. The Spanish governor, when warned that Blake would attack, answered tartly: “Get you gone, if you will, and let Blake come, if he dares.”

Blake called a council of war. It was resolved to destroy the fleet, though it was impossible to bring the ships off. He sent Captain Stayner with a squadron to force the passage into the bay while the other frigates played on the forts and lines. Stayner’s squadron was quickly supported by Blake with the whole fleet, who boarded the Spanish galleons, made himself master of them all, and set them on fire. The entire Spanish fleet was burnt to the water’s edge, except two ships that sank outright. Then, the wind shifting to the south-west, Blake passed with his fleet safely out of the bay again, losing no more than forty-eight men killed and about one hundred and twenty wounded.

“It was without question the boldest undertaking of its kind that had ever been performed,” writes Campbell. “The Spaniards, who are romantic enough in their own conduct, were so much astonished at his, that they quite lost their spirits, and thenceforward never thought themselves safe either from numbers or fortifications.”

Lord Clarendon’s assessment became the classic verdict: “He was the first man who brought ships to contemn castles on shore, which had been thought ever very formidable, and were discovered by him to make a noise only, and to fright those who could be rarely hurt by them. He was the first that infused that proportion of courage into the seamen, by making them see by experience what mighty things they could do, if they were resolved; and taught them to fight in fire, as well as upon water.”

Death at Sea

After Santa Cruz, Blake cruised again off Cadiz. His health, which had suffered from three continuous years at sea, was failing — a complication of dropsy and scurvy. He resolved to sail for England. As his ship, the St George, entered Plymouth Sound on 17 August 1657, he expired within sight of his native land. He was about fifty-nine years of age.

His body was embalmed, his bowels buried at Plymouth, and his corpse conveyed to Greenwich, whence the protector ordered a magnificent funeral procession to Westminster Abbey. He was interred in the chapel of Henry VII. At the Restoration, his body was removed from the abbey with others buried during the troubles; it was decently reinterred in the churchyard. Though a republican by principle, he was esteemed even by the royalists. Hume records his characteristic remark to his seamen: “It is still our duty to fight for our country, into what hands soever the government may fall.”

Legacy: Founder of England’s Professional Navy

Robert Blake transformed the English navy from an auxiliary arm into a formidable instrument of national power. Before him, English admirals had been cautious, valuing above all the safety of their ships; Blake taught the navy to fight aggressively, to attack fortifications, and to take risks for decisive results. He was the first to show that ships could engage coastal fortifications on equal terms, a lesson that would be taken up by Nelson at Copenhagen and by every great admiral who followed.

The Commonwealth period, with Blake, Deane, Monk, Penn, and Lawson, created the professional officer corps — men raised by merit rather than birth — that would carry the Royal Navy through the wars of the following century. Blake’s fusion of military discipline with naval command — his use of soldiers on board ship, his tactical innovations in line-of-battle fighting — laid the foundations of England’s maritime supremacy. Dr Bate’s epitaph sums it: “He humbled the pride of France, reduced Portugal to reason, broke the naval force of Holland, and drove them to the shelter of their ports, suppressed the rovers of Barbary, and twice triumphed over Spain.”
