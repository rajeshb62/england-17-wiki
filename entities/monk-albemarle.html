George Monck, 1st Duke of Albemarle

Summary

George Monck (1608–1670), 1st Duke of Albemarle, was the most enigmatic figure of the English Revolution. A soldier who fought for the king, then for the parliament, then for the Protectorate, then single-handedly restored the monarchy — Monck’s career was a study in pragmatic loyalty. He commanded Cromwell’s army in Scotland, held the nation together after the Protector’s death, marched his Coldstream troops to London in 1660, and orchestrated the Restoration of Charles II with a minimum of bloodshed. Hume, who thought him a man of parts “more solid than shining,” observed that his decisive intervention “fortune, united to prudence, had enabled him to perform.” Created Duke of Albemarle, he went on to command the English fleet in the epic Four Days’ Battle of 1666 against the Dutch, an old soldier learning the sea at fifty-eight and fighting De Ruyter to a standstill. He died in 1670, leaving his reputation to the verdict of a grateful — and puzzled — nation.

Early Life and Military Apprenticeship

George Monck was born on 6 December 1608 at Potheridge, near Great Torrington in Devon, the second son of Sir Thomas Monck, a gentleman of ancient but reduced family. Lacking a settled estate, he sought his fortune by the sword — the common resource of younger sons in an age of incessant war. At sixteen he enlisted as a volunteer in the expedition to Cadiz (1625), and saw his first service in the disastrous descent upon the Spanish coast. He then served under Sir Richard Grenville and Lord Wimbledon in the Low Countries, where the Dutch war with Spain provided a harsh schooling in military discipline. In the Dutch service he fought at the famous Siege of Breda (1637) and rose to the rank of captain, learning the trade of war under the eyes of the finest soldiers in Europe.

Returning to England in 1638, he obtained a commission in the army being raised by Charles I against the Scots in the First Bishops’ War. His conduct in the sorry campaign of 1639 brought him to the notice of the Earl of Strafford, who appointed him lieutenant-colonel of a regiment in the Irish army.

The Civil War: Royalist, Prisoner, Parliamentarian

When the Civil War broke out in 1642, Monck naturally adhered to the king. He fought with the royalist forces in Ireland, where the rebellion of 1641 had already plunged the kingdom into confusion. But the king’s Irish army, cut off from reinforcement and command, was defeated by the parliamentary forces at Nantwich in January 1644. Monck was taken prisoner and sent to the Tower of London.

His imprisonment was not severe, and the parliament was eager to recruit capable officers. Monck, whose loyalty was to the profession of arms rather than to any party, accepted a commission in the parliamentary army. He served under General Fairfax with distinction, commanding a regiment of foot at the storming of Naseby (1645) and in the reduction of the west country. His military competence, discipline, and total lack of political enthusiasm marked him as a useful instrument — one who would obey orders without question.

Cromwell’s Commander in Scotland

In 1650, Cromwell appointed Monck as his lieutenant-general in Scotland, with the rank of colonel of foot in the New Model Army. He commanded a brigade at the Battle of Dunbar (3 September 1650), where Cromwell’s crushing victory over the Scots established the Commonwealth’s authority north of the border. After Cromwell departed for England, Monck remained as commander-in-chief in Scotland, reducing the remaining strongholds. He laid siege to Stirling Castle, which surrendered with all the records of the kingdom, and forced the submission of the Earl of Leven and other royalist lords. Hume records that “the successes which attended Monk in Scotland were no less decisive” than those of Cromwell himself. By 1654 he had completed the subjugation of the Highlands, building a chain of forts and governing the kingdom with a firm but not brutal hand.

His command in Scotland lasted nearly a decade. During those years he kept his army disciplined, his administration orderly, and his opinions opaque. He impressed no political character upon his soldiers — a remarkable feat in an army that had become a debating society for republicans, Fifth Monarchists, and Levellers. To all appearances, he was Cromwell’s most reliable subordinate.

The Protectorate Collapse and the March to London

When Oliver Cromwell died in September 1658, Monck immediately proclaimed Richard Cromwell as Protector and held Scotland quiet. But the new Protector’s regime collapsed within months, and the Rump Parliament was restored in May 1659. The army in England, faction-ridden and mutinous, expelled the Rump in October, and a Committee of Safety seized power. Monck, in Scotland, watched and waited.

From his headquarters at Coldstream on the Scottish border, Monck began his march south in November 1659. He declared for the parliament against the army’s Committee of Safety, and the country — exhausted by years of military rule — rallied to his standard. Lambert, sent north with a superior army to intercept him, found his own troops melting away; Monck’s discipline and reputation for fair dealing had preceded him.

He crossed the Tweed at Coldstream on New Year’s Day 1660 — the origin of the Coldstream Guards, the oldest continuously serving regiment in the British army — and marched slowly towards London. The Rump, restored to power by his advance, received him with fulsome thanks. But Monck had his own purposes. On 6 February 1660 he entered London and quartered his army at Westminster. The Rump, desperate to retain power, ordered him to suppress the City of London — to break down its gates and portcullises, and arrest its leading citizens. Monck obeyed in a rough show of force, then turned on his masters.

The Restoration of the Monarchy

On 11 February 1660, Monck wrote a letter to the parliament demanding writs for a new and free parliament within a week. He marched into the City and, at Guildhall, swore union with the citizens for the settlement of the commonwealth. The joy, Hume records, was universal: “The prospect of peace, concord, liberty, justice, broke forth at once from amidst the deepest darkness.” City bonfires roasted rumps in effigy; the long parliament was, in the popular mind, buried.

On 21 February, Monck readmitted the secluded members — those Presbyterians and moderate parliamentarians whom Pride’s Purge had expelled in 1648. They dissolved the Long Parliament for good and issued writs for a Convention Parliament. Monck, through his kinsman Morrice and Sir John Granville, opened a secret correspondence with Charles II in exile at Breda. The king issued the Declaration of Breda (April 1660), promising a general pardon, liberty of conscience, and satisfaction of army arrears. The Convention Parliament, overwhelmingly royalist, voted unanimously to restore the monarchy.

Charles landed at Dover on 25 May 1660. Monck met him on the beach. The king created him Duke of Albemarle, Knight of the Garter, and granted him extensive lands and offices. Hume notes that Monck, alone among the great men of the Revolution, was never suspected of aiming at the crown for himself: “His caution, reserve, and consummate prudence in the most critical junctures, were the qualities that most signalised his character.”

Naval Command and the Second Dutch War

In the Second Dutch War (1665–1667), the old soldier was called to a new element. On 23 April 1666, Albemarle went on board the fleet as joint commander with Prince Rupert. It was an extraordinary appointment: Monck was fifty-eight years old, had never commanded at sea, and was setting out to fight the greatest admiral of the age — Michiel de Ruyter.

Campbell records that Albemarle, like Blake before him, “thought that fighting was, almost on any terms, preferable to running away.” When intelligence arrived that the French fleet was sailing from Toulon to join the Dutch, Albemarle insisted on detaching Prince Rupert with twenty ships to intercept them. Sir George Ayscue protested against the temerity of fighting the Dutch with only sixty sail, but Albemarle’s authority prevailed.

The Four Days’ Battle (1–4 June 1666) followed — the longest naval engagement of the age of sail. The English had sixty ships against ninety-one Dutch, carrying 4,716 guns and 22,460 men. On the first day, Albemarle attacked before the Dutch could weigh anchor, and both fleets fought until nightfall. The second day saw Van Tromp surrounded and De Ruyter himself in desperate straits. On the third day, Albemarle was forced to retreat, burning three disabled ships and conducting the withdrawal with, in Campbell’s words, “wonderful courage and conduct.” He burnt the ships that were disabled, sailed before with those most torn, and brought up the rear with twenty-eight men-of-war. On the fourth day, with Prince Rupert rejoined, the fleets fought again until both were crippled. The outcome was a costly draw: the English lost sixteen ships, the Dutch fifteen.

Hume calls it “one of the most memorable battles that we read of in story,” and judges that Albemarle “made here some atonement by his valour for the rashness of the attempt.” The Dutch Pensioner De Witte said that “if the English were beat, their defeat did them more honour than all their former victories.”

On 25 July 1666, Albemarle and Rupert fought the St James’s Day Fight, a clear English victory. De Ruyter’s squadron was battered and forced to retreat; Admirals Evertz, De Vries, and Koenders were killed. The battle restored English prestige after the Four Days’ Battle, though the war as a whole ended in humiliation when De Ruyter broke the chain at Chatham and burned the fleet in the Medway (June 1667).

Death and Character

George Monck, Duke of Albemarle, died on 3 January 1670 at New Hall in Essex, aged sixty-one. He was buried in Westminster Abbey — a singular honour for a man who had fought against the king’s father, commanded the republic’s army, and then restored the monarchy. His death occasioned no controversy; he had outlived his enemies and kept his friends.

His character resists easy summary. He was not a man of brilliant intellect — Hume thought his capacity “not extensive” and that he appeared “unfit for the court, a scene of life to which he had never been accustomed.” But his judgment in the critical hour was unerring. He understood the one thing that no other commander in the revolutionary wars understood: that the army could not govern England, and that the only settlement the nation would accept was the return of the king. He had the patience to wait for that hour, the nerve to act when it came, and the modesty to step aside afterwards.

Campbell, who wrote his naval Memoirs with admiration, says simply that Albemarle was of the same spirit as Blake — a man who believed that the reputation of a maritime nation depended on fighting. Hume calls him “a man of plain sense, great integrity, and invincible firmness.” His political philosophy, if he had one, was fidelity to the established order — first to the king, then to the parliament, then to the Protector, and finally back to the king. He served every master faithfully while they governed, and abandoned them only when they had ceased to be the government.

Monck’s greatest achievement was the Restoration itself: a bloodless counter-revolution carried out by an army that could have turned England into a military dictatorship. That he did not seize the crown, that he restored it instead, is the measure of the man. He was — to borrow his own phrase — the instrument of providence; but he was also, in an age of fanatics and ideologues, a practical man who knew what England needed and had the courage to give it to her.
