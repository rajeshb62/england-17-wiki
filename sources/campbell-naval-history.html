Campbell — Naval History of Great Britain, Vol. I

John Campbell (1708–1775) — The Naval History of Great Britain, Commencing with the Earliest Period of History, and Continued to the Expedition against Algiers, under the Command of Lord Exmouth, in 1816. Including the History and Lives of the British Admirals. — Printed for Baldwyn and Co., London, 1818.

This is Volume I of an 8-volume set. It covers English naval history from the ancient Britons (pre-Roman period) through the death of King James I in 1625. The volume contains 618 page-files (OCR’d from the original scan), extracted as 24,529 lines (~1.1 MB) of plain text. The text is OCR output from a 19th-century printed book, so minor artifacts (scrambled characters, odd line breaks) are present but do not impede reading.

Scope note: Only Volume I has been acquired (covering antiquity–1625). Volumes II–VIII (1625–1816) are not yet available. For the 17th-century wiki, the most directly relevant chapter is Chapter XIII (James I, 1603–1625).

The book includes portrait plates of admirals and battle plan engravings, which were not extractable from the OCR’d text.

Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

CHAP. I — Naval History of the Ancient Britons (line 1713)

Covers the maritime activities of the Britons before the Roman invasion, during the Roman occupation of Britain, and in the post-Roman period up to the coming of the Saxons. Examines the evidence for early British sea power from Geoffrey of Monmouth and other early chronicles, discusses the Roman fleet in British waters, and the gradual decline of Roman naval presence. Draws on Camden, the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, and classical authors including Caesar and Tacitus. Spans approximately 1,740 years.

CHAP. II — Naval History of the Saxons (line 2822)

Traces the naval history of the Anglo-Saxons from their first settlement in Britain to their subjugation by the Danes. Includes extensive memoirs of King Alfred the Great, recognised as the founder of the English navy. Covers Alfred’s shipbuilding program, his naval battles against the Danes, and the organisation of the first standing fleet. Also details the reigns of Alfred’s successors and the deteriorating naval situation that allowed Danish incursions.

CHAP. III — Naval History of the Danes (line 3906)

From the peaceful accession of Canute (Cnut) to the English throne, through the restoration of the Saxon line under Edward the Confessor, to the death of King Harold at Hastings in 1066. Discusses Danish naval organisation, Canute’s empire spanning England, Denmark, and Norway, and the naval aspects of the Norman Conquest. Ends with the Battle of Hastings and the collapse of Anglo-Saxon naval power.

CHAP. IV — Naval History under the Norman Kings (line 4633)

Covers the reigns of William the Conqueror, William Rufus, Henry I (Beauclerk), and Stephen. Examines William’s use of naval power for the Conquest, the organisation of the Cinque Ports, naval administration under the Norman dynasty, and the maritime dimensions of the Anarchy under Stephen. Details ship types, naval taxation, and the beginnings of English naval administration.

CHAP. V — Naval History under the Early Plantagenets (line 5690)

A broad chapter covering the reigns from Henry II through Richard II (1154–1399). Discusses naval operations during the Crusades (especially Richard I’s voyages), King John’s loss of the fleet and the French invasion threat, Henry III’s naval rebuilding, the naval wars with France under Edward I, II, and III — including the Battle of Sluys (1340), the first great English naval victory of the Hundred Years’ War — and the naval decline under Richard II.

CHAP. VI — Naval History under the House of Lancaster (line 8935)

Covers the reigns of Henry IV, Henry V, and Henry VI (1399–1461). Focuses on Henry V’s naval buildup for the invasion of France (Agincourt campaign), the naval blockade of Harfleur, and the English fleet’s dominance in the Channel. Includes memoirs of Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick (“the Kingmaker”) and Sir Anthony Widville, Earl Rivers, discussing their roles as naval commanders during the Wars of the Roses. The chapter ends with the deterioration of English naval power amid the dynastic conflict.

CHAP. VII — Naval History under the House of York (line 9670)

Covers the brief reigns of Edward IV, Edward V, and Richard III (1461–1485). Examines Edward IV’s restoration of naval order after the Wars of the Roses, his encouragement of commerce, and the beginnings of English overseas trade expansion. Richard III’s naval preparations against the Tudor invasion are discussed, culminating in Henry Tudor’s landing at Milford Haven and the end of the Yorkist dynasty.

CHAP. VIII — Naval History under Henry VII (line 11276)

Covers 1485–1509. Henry VII’s establishment of a royal navy, including the construction of the Great Harry (the first English carrack). Chronicles the voyages of John Cabot (discovery of Newfoundland, 1497) and the beginning of English Atlantic exploration. Includes memoirs of Sir John Cabot and Sir Edward Howard, detailing early Tudor naval administration and the founding of the permanent royal dockyards at Portsmouth.

CHAP. IX — Naval History under Henry VIII (line 11920)

The longest chapter on a single reign to this point (1509–1547). Henry VIII’s massive naval expansion: the building of the Mary Rose, Great Harry, and dozens of other warships; the establishment of the Navy Board and the Admiralty; the construction of dockyards at Deptford and Woolwich; and the naval war with France and Scotland. Includes memoirs of Sir Thomas Howard, Duke of Norfolk (Lord Admiral) and Sir William Fitz-Williams, Earl of Southampton. The sinking of the Mary Rose in 1545 is covered.

CHAP. X — Naval History under Edward VI (line 13008)

Covers 1547–1553. The continuation of Henry VIII’s naval policies under the boy-king, including the voyage of Sebastian Cabot (who led an expedition seeking a Northeast Passage) and Sir Hugh Willoughby’s ill-fated Arctic expedition (1553), whose entire crew perished. Discusses the decline of naval readiness during the Somerset and Northumberland protectorates.

CHAP. XI — Naval History under Queen Mary (line 14165)

Covers 1553–1558. English naval involvement in the Spanish marriage, the war with France that resulted in the loss of Calais (1558), and the decay of the fleet under Mary’s pro-Spanish policies. Includes memoirs of Sir John Dudley, Duke of Northumberland, Charles Howard, Earl of Nottingham (later Lord Admiral under Elizabeth), Robert Devereux, Earl of Essex (naval commander against Spain), Sir Humphrey Gilbert (explorer and naval theorist), Sir John Hawkins (slave trader, naval treasurer, ship designer), Sir Francis Drake (circumnavigator and admiral), and Sir Martin Frobisher (Arctic explorer and naval commander against the Armada).

CHAP. XII — Naval History under Queen Elizabeth I (line 14907)

The most substantial chapter in the volume (covers lines 14907–20918, ~6,000 lines). Covers 1558–1603 in immense detail. Key topics:

  • The naval buildup in the 1570s–1580s in anticipation of war with Spain
  • The voyages of discovery: Thomas Cavendish (circumnavigation), Edward Fenton, George Clifford, Earl of Cumberland, Sir Robert Dudley
  • Sir Richard Grenville and the Revenge at the Azores (1591)
  • Sir Richard Hawkins’s South Sea voyage (1593)
  • Captain James Lancaster’s East India voyage (1591–1594)
  • Captain William Parker’s West Indian expeditions
  • The Spanish Armada (1588): full campaign narrative — fleet composition, battle tactics, the fireship attack at Gravelines, the disastrous return voyage around Scotland and Ireland
  • The Counter-Armada (1589) and the Portugal expedition
  • Privateering and commerce raiding as state policy
  • The establishment of the East India Company (1600)
  • Naval administration under Lord Admiral Howard and John Hawkins (as Treasurer)

CHAP. XIII — Naval History under King James I (line 20919)

Covers 1603–1625. This is the chapter most directly relevant to the 17th-century wiki. Key topics:

  • The peaceful accession of James VI of Scotland to the English throne and the political manoeuvring that secured the succession (Cecil’s secret correspondence with James, the Howards’ support)
  • The immediate proclamation of James and his journey to London
  • Naval security during the transition — the fleet kept on station off Spain to prevent disruption
  • The peace with Spain (1604) and its effect on the navy: decline of the fleet, sale of ships, decay of dockyards
  • The Gunpowder Plot (1605) and its naval connections
  • The Virginia Company and the founding of Jamestown (1607) — naval logistics of the colony
  • English exploration and colonisation under James: Newfoundland, the Somers Isles (Bermuda)
  • Sir Walter Raleigh’s expeditions to Guiana (1595, 1617) and his execution (1618)
  • The decline of English privateering after the peace with Spain
  • Naval administration under the Duke of Buckingham (Lord High Admiral from 1619)
  • The disastrous Cadiz expedition (1625 — promised in the next volume)
  • State of the navy at James’s death: corruption, neglected ships, unpaid seamen

The volume ends mid-sentence promising treatment of Buckingham’s management in the next volume.
