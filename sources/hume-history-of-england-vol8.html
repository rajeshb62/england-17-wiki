Hume — History of England, Vol. VIII

David Hume (1711–1776) — The History of England from the Invasion of Julius Caesar to the Revolution in 1688. Vol. VIII. — Printed by J. F. Dove for Baynes and Son, London, 1822.

This is Volume VIII — the final volume — of Hume’s 8-volume History of England. It covers the last years of Charles II (1674–1685), the reign of James II (1685–1688), and the Glorious Revolution (1688–1689), ending with Hume’s concluding essay on manners and arts.

Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

CHAP. LXVI — Charles II (page 1)

Covers 1674–1678. The aftermath of the Cabal ministry. The schemes of the Cabal exposed; Sir William Temple’s remonstrances. The campaign of 1674 — the Dutch War ends, the Peace of Westminster. A parliament: the debate on passive obedience vs. resistance. The campaign of 1675 — the French advance in Flanders. The Congress of Nimeguen (1676–1678) — the general peace negotiations. The uncertain conduct of Charles: his secret French subsidy from Louis XIV, his public pro-Dutch stance. The campaign of 1677. Parliament’s distrust of the king. The marriage of William of Orange to the Lady Mary (Nov 1677) — the Protestant alliance cemented. Plans for peace; negotiations. The campaign of 1678. The Peace of Nimeguen — France gains Franche-Comté, the Dutch satisfied. The state of affairs in Scotland — the Duke of Lauderdale’s repressive rule.

CHAP. LXVII — Charles II (page 60)

Covers 1678–1679. The Popish Plot — the most extraordinary political crisis of Charles II’s reign. Titus Oates’s narrative: his story of a Jesuit conspiracy to murder the king, burn London, and impose Catholicism. Oates’s character — Hume’s skeptical portrait of the informer. Coleman’s letters — a real correspondence with the French Jesuit confessor, suggesting Catholic hopes. Sir Edmund Berry Godfrey’s murder (Oct 1678) — the magistrate who took Oates’s deposition found dead, the nation convulsed. General consternation: the search for conspirators, the arrest of Catholic peers (including Lord Stafford, Arundel, Powis). The parliament: its zeal against Popery. Bedloe’s narrative — corroboration of the plot. The accusation of Danby, the Lord Treasurer, for allegedly negotiating with France without parliament’s knowledge. Danby’s impeachment and fall.

CHAP. LXVIII — Charles II (page 114)

Covers 1679. The Popish Plot hysteria at its peak. The execution of William Staley, Coleman, Ireland, Grove, and other accused plotters. The trial and execution of Lord Stafford (Dec 1680) — the last Catholic peer judicially murdered. The crisis of the Exclusion Bill: Shaftesbury and the Green Ribbon Club move to exclude the Duke of York (a Catholic) from the succession. The parliament passes the Exclusion Bill in the Commons; Charles dissolves parliament. The Habeas Corpus Act (1679) passed in this session. The king’s health; the fear of a Catholic succession. The struggle over the militia: who controls the army? The second general election of 1679 — the “Exclusion Parliaments.” Charles rejects exclusion, begins to reassert his authority.

CHAP. LXIX — Charles II (page 155)

Covers 1680–1685. The Oxford Parliament (March 1681) — Charles summons parliament to Oxford (a royalist stronghold), the Exclusion Bill re-introduced, Charles dissolves parliament after one week and never calls another. The Tory reaction: the tide turns against the Whigs. The Rye House Plot (1683) — a Whig conspiracy to assassinate Charles and his brother. The discovery and suppression of the conspirators: Lord Russell, Algernon Sidney, the Duke of Monmouth implicated. The trial and execution of Lord Russell — his dignified death. The execution of Algernon Sidney — based on his unpublished republican writings. The trial of Monmouth — he confesses, is pardoned, and exiled. The king’s authority restored; the Tory ascendancy; the attacks on the corporation charters.

CHAP. LXX — James II (page 206)

Covers 1685–1688. The accession of James II (Feb 1685) — his speech to the council promising to preserve the constitution. Monmouth’s Rebellion (June 1685): the landing at Lyme Regis, the popular support in the West Country. The Battle of Sedgemoor (6 July 1685) — James’s army under Feversham and Churchill crushes the rebels. The Bloody Assizes: Judge Jeffreys’s brutal punishment of the rebels. The execution of Monmouth. James’s Catholicising policies: the dispensing power used to appoint Catholics to office. The case of Godden v. Hales — the legal validation of the dispensing power. The state of Ireland: the Catholic Earl of Tyrconnell put in charge. The breach between the king and the Church of England. The Court of Ecclesiastical Commission revived. The suspension of the penal laws and the Declaration of Indulgence (1687). The attempt on Magdalen College, Oxford — the king’s demand for a Catholic president resisted. The trial and acquittal of the Seven Bishops (June 1688) — the great popular triumph. The birth of the Prince of Wales (June 1688) — the prospect of a Catholic dynasty. The Invitation to William of Orange.

CHAP. LXXI — William III (page 264)

Covers 1688–1689. The Glorious Revolution. The conduct of the Prince of Orange: his league against France, his refusal to concur with James’s policies. The invitation from the Immortal Seven — the Whig and Tory lords who ask William to intervene. William’s preparations: his fleet and army assembled. Louis XIV’s offers to James — rejected. James’s desperate attempts to conciliate: the concessions to the Church, the repeal of the Test Act, the dismissal of Catholic officers. William’s fleet sails (Oct 1688), driven back by storms. The second sailing: the “Protestant Wind” carries William’s armada to Torbay. William lands at Brixham (5 Nov 1688). James’s army — the defection of Churchill and other officers. James’s flight: his first attempt to escape, his capture, his second flight to France. The Convention Parliament. The Declaration of Rights. The offer of the crown to William and Mary (Feb 1689). The Revolution settlement: the Bill of Rights, the Toleration Act. The volume concludes with Hume’s assessment of the Revolution.

MANNERS AND ARTS (page 307)

Hume’s concluding essay on the state of English society, culture, and commerce during the 17th century. Topics include: the progress of learning, the growth of science (the Royal Society, Newton, Boyle, Locke), the state of literature (Milton, Dryden, Butler’s Hudibras), the improvements in commerce and navigation, the rise of the public credit, the changes in manners and social life. A comprehensive assessment of the century’s civilising process.

The volume also contains a detailed index to all eight volumes (pages 447–460).
