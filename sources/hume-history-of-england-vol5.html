Hume — History of England, Vol. V

David Hume (1711–1776) — The History of England from the Invasion of Julius Caesar to the Revolution in 1688. To Which is Prefixed, a Short Account of His Life, Written by Himself. A New Edition, in Eight Volumes. Vol. V. — Printed by J. F. Dove for Baynes and Son, London, 1822.

This is Volume V of David Hume’s 8-volume History of England. It covers the latter half of Elizabeth I’s reign (from c. 1562) through the early years of James I (to c. 1612).

The volume contains 508 page-files (OCR’d from the University of Chicago Library copy, shelfmark 12846423), extracted as 21,324 lines (~1 MB) of plain text.

Scope note for the 17th-century wiki: Only the final ~60 pages (Chaps. XLV–XLVI, covering James I from 1603 to ~1612) fall within the 17th century. The bulk of the volume is Elizabethan (16th century), though the Appendix III essay on government, revenue, and commerce is relevant background for understanding early Stuart governance.

Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

CHAP. XXXIX — Elizabeth (line 119, page 1)

Covers c. 1562–1568. Opens with a survey of Europe after the Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis — Philip II of Spain and Elizabeth as the great rival powers. The French Wars of Religion begin; Elizabeth takes Havre de Grace as security, but it is lost to plague and French Catholic forces. Affairs in Scotland: Mary Queen of Scots marries Darnley, the murder of Rizzio, the murder of Darnley, Mary’s marriage to Bothwell, insurrections, Mary’s imprisonment and flight into England. Conferences at York and Hampton Court to adjudicate the charges against Mary.

CHAP. XL — Elizabeth (line 3,882, page 87)

Covers c. 1568–1572. The character of the Puritans and the rise of religious dissent. The Duke of Norfolk’s conspiracy to marry Mary Queen of Scots. The Northern Rebellion (1569) — Catholic earls rising in the North. Assassination of the Earl of Murray (Regent of Scotland). Parliament and the succession question. The French Wars of Religion continue. Affairs of the Low Countries and the Dutch Revolt. Norfolk’s second conspiracy, his trial and execution (1572). The Massacre of St Bartholomew (1572) and its impact on English politics.

CHAP. XLI — Elizabeth (line 7,165, page 164)

Covers c. 1572–1585. Affairs of Scotland — the regency of Morton, James VI’s minority. Spanish affairs and the growing rivalry with England. Sir Francis Drake’s circumnavigation (1577–1580) and his raids on Spanish America. Parliament and the succession debate. Negotiations for Elizabeth’s marriage to the Duke of Anjou. The Scottish Reformation and the fall of Morton. Mary’s letters and conspiracies. The ecclesiastical commission. Affairs of the Low Countries — English intervention in the Dutch Revolt. Open hostilities with Spain begin. The assassination of William the Silent (1584).

CHAP. XLII — Elizabeth (line 9,267, page 214)

Covers c. 1585–1588. The climax of Elizabeth’s reign — the crisis with Mary Queen of Scots and the Spanish Armada. Babington’s conspiracy (1586) and Mary’s fatal correspondence. The trial of Mary at Fotheringhay, the sentence, James VI’s intercession, and Mary’s execution (8 Feb 1587). Elizabeth’s affected sorrow. Drake’s raid on Cadiz (1587), “singeing the King of Spain’s beard.” Philip II’s project to invade England. The Spanish Armada (1588): English preparations, the Armada’s arrival in the Channel, the running battles from Plymouth to Gravelines, the fireship attack, the Armada’s disastrous flight around Scotland and Ireland. Parliament and thanksgiving. The expedition against Portugal (1589) — Drake and Norris’s failure.

CHAP. XLIII — Elizabeth (line 12,658, page 292)

Covers c. 1589–1598. French affairs: the murder of the Duke of Guise (1588), the murder of Henry III (1589), the accession of Henry IV of Navarre. English naval enterprises against Spain — privateering, the Azores voyages. Henry IV’s conversion to Catholicism (“Paris is worth a mass”). Scottish affairs: the struggle between James VI and the Presbyterian kirk. Naval enterprises continued — the Earl of Cumberland, Sir Richard Hawkins. The Peace of Vervins (1598) between France and Spain. The Earl of Essex’s rising influence.

CHAP. XLIV — Elizabeth (line 14,409, page 333)

Covers c. 1598–1603. The final years. State of Ireland: the Tyrone rebellion (Nine Years’ War). Essex sent to Ireland (1599), his disastrous campaign, his unauthorized return to England. Essex’s disgrace, his intrigues, his abortive insurrection (1601), his trial and execution. Mountjoy’s success in Ireland: the defeat of the Spaniards and Irish at Kinsale (1601), Tyrone’s submission. Parliament and the monopolies debate. Elizabeth’s final sickness, her death (24 March 1603), and her character — one of Hume’s most famous set-pieces, praising her firmness, intelligence, and mastery of her kingdom.

APPENDIX III — Elizabethan Government, Revenue, Commerce, etc. (line 16,838, pages 333–429)

A thematic essay covering the state of England during Elizabeth’s reign. Not a narrative but a structural analysis:

  • Government — the prerogative, Star Chamber, the ecclesiastical commission, the abuse of power under colour of law (imprisonment without trial, press gangs, monopolies)
  • Revenues — crown lands, parliamentary supply, the sale of monopolies, Elizabeth’s parsimony contrasted with her successors’ difficulties
  • Commerce — the East India Company (founded 1600), the Hanseatic League’s decay, the Merchant Adventurers, the growth of English shipping
  • Military force — the militia system, the navy, the artillery, the ammunition shortage
  • Manufactures — the low state of English manufacturing compared to foreign imports
  • Learning — the flourishing of English literature under Elizabeth (Spenser, Shakespeare), the state of the universities
  • Prices — the rise in prices during Elizabeth’s reign, the influx of American silver
  • Manners — the decay of the aristocracy, the growth of the gentry and middle classes

The appendix concludes with a reflection on Spenser’s Faerie Queene and the peculiarities of Elizabethan literary taste, then transitions to the Stuart era.

CHAP. XLV — James I (line 18,709, page 431)

Covers 1603–c. 1606. The accession of James VI of Scotland to the English throne — the smooth transition from Tudor to Stuart. Key topics:

  • The Accession — the peaceful succession, James’s journey from Edinburgh to London, the shower of honours and knighthoods
  • State of Europe — the Habsburg-Bourbon rivalry, James’s vision of a peaceful foreign policy
  • Rosni’s Negotiations — the French envoy’s mission to secure James’s alliance against Spain
  • Raleigh’s Conspiracy — the Main Plot and the Bye Plot (1603): Sir Walter Raleigh, Lord Cobham, and Lord Grey of Wilton accused of treason. Raleigh’s trial, his dramatic conviction, and his imprisonment in the Tower
  • Hampton Court Conference (1604) — the meeting between the Anglican bishops, the Puritan clergy, and James I. The Puritan millenary petition, James’s famous retort to the Scottish presbyterians, the decision to produce a new translation of the Bible (the King James Version)
  • The First Parliament — the debate over the Union of England and Scotland, the Buckinghamshire election case (Goodwin v. Fortescue), the question of wardships and purveyance
  • Peace with Spain (1604) — the Treaty of London, ending the 19-year Anglo-Spanish war

CHAP. XLVI — James I (line 19,953, page 460)

Covers c. 1605–1612. Key topics:

  • The Gunpowder Plot (1605) — Hume’s dramatic narrative of the conspiracy: Robert Catesby, Guy Fawkes, the discovery of the gunpowder under the House of Lords, the pursuit and death of the conspirators, the trial of Henry Garnet (the Jesuit superior), and the harsh recusancy laws that followed
  • Parliament and the Union — the continued debate over the Anglo-Scottish union, the failure of the Great Contract (1610) — James’s proposal to exchange wardships and purveyance for a permanent parliamentary revenue, which collapsed when the Commons demanded the redress of grievances first
  • The Truce with Spain and the United Provinces (1609) — the Twelve Years’ Truce between Spain and the Dutch Republic
  • The Assassination of Henry IV (1610) — the murder of the French king by Ravaillac, the consternation in England, the fear of Catholic conspiracy
  • Arminianism — the rise of the Arminian (anti-Calvinist) party in the Church of England, James’s involvement in the Dutch Remonstrant controversy
  • State of Ireland — the Plantation of Ulster, the introduction of English law, the abolition of Irish customs (Gavelkinde, Tanistry), the suppression of the O’Dogherty rebellion
  • The volume ends with James’s first parliament (which sat for nearly seven years) dissolved in 1611, and a discussion of Irish policy extending to 1612
