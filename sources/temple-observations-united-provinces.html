Sir William Temple, Baronet (1628–1699) — Observations upon the United Provinces of the Netherlands. By Sir William Temple of Shene in the County of Surrey, Baronet, Ambassador at the Hague and at Aix-la-Chapelle, in the Year 1668. The Seventh Edition. Corrected and Augmented. — Printed for Jacob Tonson within Grays-Inn Gate next Grays-Inn Lane, and Awnsham and John Churchill at the Black Swan in Pater-Noster-Row, London, 1705.

Temple was Charles II’s ambassador at The Hague and a principal architect of the Triple Alliance (1668) with the Dutch and Sweden against France. This book is his famous contemporary anatomy of the Dutch Republic — written after two years’ embassy, and framed by the shock of the French invasion of 1672 (the Rampjaar, or “Disaster Year”), when the inland provinces fell in days. For this England wiki it is mainly a window on how English diplomacy and trade writers understood their Dutch rival and ally.

Contents

The Preface (line 120 / page-file 13)

Temple opens from the contrast of 1672: a state that had reached “prodigious Growth in Riches, Beauty, Extent of Commerce, and Number of Inhabitants,” envied and feared by its neighbours, then “almost ruin’d and broken to pieces, in some few Days, and by very few Blows.” Inland provinces swallowed “almost as sudden, and unresisted, as the Inundations” that threaten the maritime ones. He writes as a former ambassador required to prepare a formal account after two years at The Hague, now offering private readers a view of the rise, the causes of greatness, and the steps toward the fall — motions “little taken Notice of by common Eyes.”

The Contents (line 482 / page-file 24)

Eight chapters as printed: Rise and Progress; Government; Situation; People and Dispositions; Religion; Trade; Forces and Revenues; Causes of their Fall in 1672.

Chap. I — Of the Rise and Progress of the United Provinces (line 502 / page-file 25)

Long narrative history of the Low Countries from late-Roman and Burgundian antiquity through Habsburg union under Charles V and Philip II. Temple traces the revolt from the Duchess of Parma’s government and Alva’s repression — Inquisition, executions, exile, Sea Beggars, the Prince of Orange — through the Union of Utrecht, Parma’s recovery of the southern provinces, Maurice and the English under Sir Francis Vere (including Newport), the turn to oceanic trade after loss of Spanish and Straits commerce, the Twelve Years’ Truce, and the later balance with Spain. He insists greatness is to be sought less in battlefield events than in “Institutions and Orders.” Ends with counsel against buying peace by yielding interest or honour, which only invites new injuries.

Chap. II — Of their Government (line 3969 / page-file 115)

The classic English anatomy of the federal republic. Temple denies it is a single commonwealth: it is a confederacy of seven sovereign provinces, each composed of cities that retain marks of sovereignty (especially criminal justice). States-General cannot make war, peace, alliance, or money levies without every province; provincial states cannot conclude those points without each voting city. Coinage alone is reserved to the Union by common agreement. He walks the “main Ingredients”: freedom of the cities; sovereignty of the provinces; constitutions of the Union; and the authority of the Princes of Orange (captain-general and admiral, military patronage, pardons, choosing magistrates from towns’ triple nominations). Detailed description of Holland’s States at The Hague, the Council of State, revenue administration (he mentions a figure on the order of £1,600,000 sterling a year without claiming a precise computation), and the habit of a “Head subordinate to their Laws and Customs.”

Chap. III — Of their Situation (line 6020 / page-file 169)

Geography as destiny. Holland, Zealand, Friesland, and Groningen on the sea are “the Strength and Greatness of this State”; the other three provinces plus conquered towns in Brabant, Flanders, and Cleves are “Outworks or Frontiers.” Temple discusses Rhine, Meuse (Mose), and Scheldt mouths, sanding, inundations, dikes, canals, the Zuyder Zee, and how inland navigation links Germany’s commodities to Dutch ports — and how the same waters force endless charge for fences and repairs.

Chap. IV — Of their People and Dispositions (line 6521 / page-file 182)

Social typology of Holland: Boors (cultivators), Schippers (mariners), Merchants, Renteeners (rentiers living on interest of inherited estates in the chief cities), and Gentlemen. Character sketches of each class — the boors’ plain dealing and milk-and-roots diet; the mariners’ roughness learned from winds and waves; mercantile thrift and industry — aimed at explaining political temper as much as manners.

Chap. V — Of their Religion (line 7719 / page-file 213)

Temple writes “not here… as a Divine, but as a mere Secular Man.” Famous defence of toleration as civil policy: belief is no more in a man’s power than his stature or feature; compelling conscience breeds disguise, not conviction. He links religious peace to the republic’s commercial and political strength, treating the plurality of confessions as a working fact of the United Provinces rather than a theological programme.

Chap. VI — Of their Trade (line 8475 / page-file 233)

Central economic argument: Holland is poor in native commodities and awkward in harbours (Texel, Zuyder Zee channels more dangerous than a voyage to Spain), yet rich because trade fills a haven, not the reverse. The Dutch become “the general Magazine of Europe” and “the common Carriers of the World” by industry, manufacture of foreign growths, and seamanship. Temple roots trade in “great multitude of People crowded into small compass of Land,” which makes necessaries dear, forces parsimony on the propertied and industry on the rest. Compares Holland (least and worst harbours/commodities) with Ireland (most and best) to separate natural endowment from commercial culture. Closes with the tidal metaphor of trade: it has a pitch above which it never rises, and loses ground in one place as it gains in another.

Chap. VII — Of their Forces and Revenues (line 9888 / page-file 271)

Military-fiscal modernity. Older Europe measured strength by native warlike subjects; standing forces were for conquerors and usurpers. Venice and Holland first changed this pattern — small land territories, weak native land armies diverted to traffic and sea, rich enough to hire stipendiary troops and trust fortification and long sieges so that wars “many times determine by force of Money, rather than of Arms.” Neighbours were forced into the same provisions. Dutch force is therefore measured by shipping, fortified places, constant revenue, and foreign bands — not by the number of native swords.

Chap. VIII — Of the Causes of their Fall in 1672 (line 10310 / page-file 283)

Temple’s post-mortem of the Disaster Year: frontier towns entered “like open Villages”; great rivers passed “as little Fords”; hearts that once faced Rome and Spain now abandoning before danger appeared. Assignable circumstances (leaving deeper causes to Providence): (1) vast trade diverting natives from arms to traffic, so later wars run by foreign mercenaries — contrast the old sieges of Haarlem, Alkmaar, and Leiden; (2) the Peace of Münster leaving them too secure by land for twenty years while attention went to the sea (two English wars; yearly Mediterranean expeditions against Algerines); (3) parsimony in reforming best foreign officers after Münster; (4) above all, the Perpetual Edict of Holland and West-Friesland after the last Prince of Orange’s death, excluding or separating the Stadtholder from the Captain-Generalship and working out Orange-party officers. Ends with the humility that wise counsel is hard before events and easy after them.

  • Temple’s embassy and the Triple Alliance (1668) — English ambassador’s vantage; defensive leagues with England and Sweden; later failure to deepen them before 1672 (Preface; Ch. VIII).
  • Anglo-Dutch wars — first English war heat (e.g. works finished in one summer); 1665 war revenues and disbanding of English troops in Dutch service; sea war as the arena that kept Dutch naval force sharp while land defence atrophied (Chs. VI–VIII).
  • 1672: England joins France — Temple treats the England–France conjunction as catastrophic for Dutch confidence (“the Name of England, joining in the War against them”); Dutch ought to have balanced France with England and Spain (Ch. VIII).
  • English staples and trade rivalry — English/Scotch staples at Dort and Rotterdam; Dutch herring on English and Scottish coasts; cloth marked with English arms; comparisons that “would of course fall to our share in England” are not automatic (Ch. VI). Pair with Child’s Discourse of Trade.
  • English as measuring-stick — Channel havens, yeomanry courage, Reformation parallels, gentry travel to England, VOC/East India company stock compared with English practice (scattered Chs. III–VII).
